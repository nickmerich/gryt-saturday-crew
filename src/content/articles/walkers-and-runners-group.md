To join a walkers and runners group without being left behind, pick a group that welcomes mixed paces, message the organizer with your pace and walk-break habits, ask how regrouping works, arrive 10 minutes early, tell the leader you are new, and start slower than you think you need to.

## Am I too slow to join a run club?

No. Slow paces and walk breaks belong in group running, and the numbers show how mainstream it has become. Road running reached 52.3 million participants in the U.S. in 2025, the second most popular outdoor activity behind hiking. The New York Road Runners community passed 100,000 members for the first time in fiscal year 2026, a 24 percent increase.

Clubs are multiplying too. Strava data shows the number of running clubs on its platform grew 3.5x in 2025. In the Mass Participation Pulse Report, 57% of women said they belong to a running club or group.

None of that proves every group welcomes walkers. It does mean the person next to you at the start line is probably not an elite. Most people there just wanted a reason to get out the door.

The fear of being too slow is mostly a fear of being last, alone, while everyone waits. You don't fix that by getting faster first. You fix it by picking a group that plans for mixed paces and asking a few direct questions before you show up. Identity beats motivation: once you run with a crew, the pace takes care of itself.

## What does 'no-drop' actually mean in practice?

"No-drop" means the group does not leave anyone behind. If you fall off the back, someone stays with you, or the group stops and waits. Nobody gets left behind.

How that works varies from group to group. Some groups assign a sweeper, a volunteer who runs at the back with the slowest person. Others use a technique often called mustering: the faster runners go ahead to a set point, such as a turnaround or trail junction, then wait until everyone catches up. Some groups simply loop back to collect people.

The term is a convention, not a guarantee. One group's "no-drop" means a dedicated sweeper at every run. Another's means a loose promise to regroup at the turnaround. Both call themselves no-drop.

So don't assume. Ask the organizer what the term means in that group: who stays with the slowest person, where the group regroups, and what happens if you need to walk. A clear answer is a good sign. A vague one tells you something too.

Step 1 below gives you the exact questions to ask.

## How do I find a group that welcomes both walkers and runners?

Start where walkers are already part of the plan. Parkrun events welcome walkers, and University of Stirling research found that introducing "parkwalker" volunteers raised walker participation at parkrun events by as much as 55.3 percent, in a study with a 31,000-person sample. That finding applies to parkrun, not to clubs in general. Frontrunners chapters are another place to look. Check each local listing for its own details.

Next, search where clubs organize. Strava reports more than 1 million clubs and 180 million users, so a club near you probably exists. That count reflects growth, not quality, and plenty of Strava clubs are built for fast training. Apps like Pace Friends and ODDS are also worth a search for local meetups.

Then compare the types of group you find:

- **Store-based clubs** usually meet at a running shop and often run on a schedule with several pace options.
- **Independent community crews** tend to be looser, with fewer rules and a more social feel.

Neither type is automatically friendlier to walkers. A shop group may offer a clear pace chart but a fast core, while a community crew may be warm but loosely organized. The only way to know is to ask, and to watch how the group treats newcomers. Look for the signals instead:

- The listing says "all paces," "walkers welcome," or "no-drop."
- Photos show people of different speeds and ages.
- The organizer answers questions quickly and specifically.
- The group meets at the same place and time every week.

That last one matters most. Consistency beats everything else, and a standing weekly appointment is easier to join than a moving target.

If you have two or three options, rank them by how easy they are to reach, not by how impressive they look. A group ten minutes from your home that meets every week will teach you more than a famous club across town that you visit once. Pick the one you can realistically attend four weeks in a row, and judge it after that, not after one run.

## Step 1: Message the organizer before you show up

Pick one session from the group's schedule, then send the organizer a short message. Someone who knows you are coming can plan for you. Someone who doesn't, can't.

Tell them your typical pace and how often you take walk breaks. Be honest, and use real numbers if you have them. "I run a minute, walk a minute" tells them more than "I'm pretty slow." Then ask who stays with the slowest person and where the group regroups.

Here is a script you can copy:

> Hi, I'm thinking of joining Saturday's run. I'm new to group running. I usually go about 13 minutes per mile and walk for a minute every ten or so. Do you run no-drop? Who stays with the slowest person, and where does the group regroup on the route? Is there a turnaround or a spot where everyone waits?

Send it a day or two ahead, not the night before. That gives the organizer time to answer and gives you time to follow up if the reply is unclear.

Keep the message short and friendly. You are not asking for permission to join, you are asking for information so you can show up prepared. Most organizers are glad to get a note like this, because it tells them you are serious about coming. If a group has no way to contact the organizer at all, that is worth noting before you decide.

If you want to ask one more thing, ask how long the route is and whether there is a shorter option. Knowing the distance in advance lets you decide ahead of time whether you will run it all, mix in walking, or turn back early.

Read the reply closely. A specific answer ("our sweeper stays at the back, and we regroup at the bridge") means the group has thought about this. A shrug or "you'll be fine" tells you little. If the answer is vague, ask a follow-up or try another group.

## Step 2: Arrive early and tell the leader you are new

Get there at least 10 minutes before the start. You find the group without a rush, and you meet the leader before the crowd forms around them.

Walk up and introduce yourself. Give your name and say it's your first run. Leaders hear that a lot, and it often leads them to pair you with a pacer or buddy for the day. Nobody will think less of you for saying it.

You do not need special gear for this, just shoes you have already worn and your phone in case you need to reach the organizer. What matters more is the conversation with the leader.

Use the extra minutes to look around. Notice how people greet each other, whether newcomers get a hello, and whether the group splits into pace clusters before the start. Those small signs tell you a lot about how the run will feel. If you see someone standing alone, say hello. They may be new too, and a shared first run is easier than a solo one.

While people arrive, use the standing-around time to find the leader or sweeper and ask where the group regroups if you are slower than the pack. Also ask who will be at the back, and whether they can point out that person before you start. If the leader says nothing about pacing, ask anyway: "Where will the group regroup if I'm slower?" A good leader will give you a spot on the route, not a shrug, and often will introduce you to someone running your pace.

## Step 3: Find your pace with the chat test

Use the chat test: run or walk at a speed where you could hold a full conversation. If you can speak in full sentences without gasping, you are in the right zone. If you manage only a word or two between breaths, slow down.

Start with a group that moves slightly slower than you think you need. Most first-timers go out too fast, because the adrenaline of a new group makes the first half mile feel easy. The aerobic engine rebuilds faster than the connective tissue, and the runners who get hurt are the ones chasing someone else's pace.

Then watch what happens. In the first half mile, a mixed group splits into clusters. The fast runners pull ahead, a middle pack forms, and others settle in at the back. Don't fight the split. Find the cluster where people are talking, and settle in with them.

Heart rate watches and pace apps can help, but you do not need them. The talk test works because it measures effort, not speed, and effort is what you can actually control on a group run. A hilly route will slow you down, and that is fine. Let the effort stay steady and let the pace move around it.

Check yourself against these signs:

- You can answer a question without pausing to breathe.
- Your breathing is steady, not ragged.
- You could keep this speed for the whole route.

If you need to walk, walk. Say "I'm taking a walk break" so the people near you know, and keep moving. A group built for mixed paces won't blink.

If you want a longer explanation of the chat test and why easy pacing works, see [conversational pace running](https://grytrunclub.com/resources/articles/conversational-pace-running).

It also helps to decide your walk-break pattern before you start. Some people walk for one minute in every ten, others walk every hill, and others walk at each regroup point. Pick a pattern, mention it to the person beside you, and stick with it for the first few runs. A plan you chose beats a break you take because you are gasping, and it keeps you from drifting off the back without anyone noticing. If the group stops to regroup, treat that stop as a free rest rather than a sign you are slow. That pause is built in for exactly this reason.

## Step 4: Stay for the coffee afterward

Stay for the social time after the run, even if you feel sweaty and a little awkward. The run is the excuse. The community happens after.

This is where belonging forms. You swap names, find out who lives near you, and hear what people are training for. That is also what gets you to come back next week, and consistency beats everything else. The numbers back up how social this is: 57% of women are members of running clubs or groups. People join crews, not only routes.

You don't need to stay long. Fifteen minutes with a cup of coffee turns a stranger into a familiar face.

If conversation feels hard at first, start with easy questions: how long have you been coming, what is the best part of the route, who else is new. People like talking about their own routine, and those answers often lead to an invitation to run together next week. Do not worry about being interesting. Being there and being friendly is enough. ## FAQs

### Am I too slow to join a run club?

No. Run clubs include people who walk, jog, and run, and group running is mainstream: road running reached 52.3 million participants in the U.S. in 2025. Pick a group that welcomes mixed paces, tell the organizer your speed ahead of time, and ask how the group regroups. Your pace matters less than showing up.

### Do I need special gear to join a community run group?

No. Wear comfortable shoes you have already used, clothes that suit the weather, and bring water. Most community groups do not require a watch, special apparel, or a membership. Avoid brand-new shoes on your first run, since fit problems show up fast over a few miles.

### Can I walk the whole thing at a run club?

At many groups, yes, but it depends on the group, so ask first. Some groups are built for walkers, and parkrun events saw walker participation rise as much as 55.3 percent after adding "parkwalker" volunteers, according to University of Stirling research. That finding is specific to parkrun, so check each group's own norms.

### How do I find a group that accommodates both running and walking?

Search parkrun, Frontrunners, Strava clubs, and local meetup apps, then message the organizer. Strava now reports more than 1 million clubs, but a large count doesn't mean every club is beginner-friendly. Look for listings that say "all paces," "walkers welcome," or "no-drop," and confirm how regrouping works.

### What if I get dropped on my first run?

Tell the leader before you start that you are new, and ask where the group regroups. If you do fall back, keep moving and stay on the route. A group that regroups at set points will catch up with you. If nobody does, treat that as information about the group and try another.

### Is it normal to feel nervous before a first group run?

Yes, almost everyone does. The nerves usually come from fear of being the slowest person. Message the organizer ahead of time, arrive early, and introduce yourself. Those three steps remove most of the unknowns, and the first few minutes of conversation usually settle the rest.

## Conclusion

You do not need to be fast to run with a group. You need a group that plans for mixed paces, a clear answer about how it regroups, and a pace you can talk at. Nobody gets left behind when you ask the right questions first.

This week, pick one group and one session, then send the organizer your pace and your regrouping questions. Show up early, say you are new, and stay for the coffee.
