A lot of runners run their easy days too fast. It's one of the most common habits in recreational running, and it quietly undermines the point of those runs. If you finish a so-called easy run feeling wiped out instead of refreshed, keep reading.

Here's what easy running means, how to tell when you've crossed the line, and how to dial it back without feeling like you're crawling.

## What "Easy Pace" Actually Means

Easy running isn't slow for the sake of slow. It's running at a low enough intensity that you can build aerobic fitness without piling up fatigue, so you can recover and run again in a day or two. In practical terms, it's a pace where you could hold a full conversation.

At first, easy pace often feels embarrassingly slow, especially if you're a former athlete or used to train hard. That's normal. It doesn't mean you're doing it wrong.

## The Simplest Check: The Talk Test

You don't need a heart rate monitor to check your easy pace. The [CDC's talk test](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/measuring/index.html) is a simple rule of thumb: at moderate intensity you can talk but not sing, and at vigorous intensity you can't say more than a few words without pausing for breath.

If you can speak in full sentences, you're in a good place for an easy run. If you're answering questions with "yeah" and "uh-huh" because anything longer takes too much effort, you're running too hard.

Try it on your next run. Say something out loud, like "I am running at an easy pace and I feel comfortable." If that comes out broken or breathless, slow down.

## Signs You're Running Your Easy Days Too Hard

### You Finish Feeling Drained, Not Refreshed

Easy runs should leave you feeling like you could have kept going. If you're dragging through the last mile or collapsing on the couch afterward, the effort was probably too high for an easy day.

### Your Breathing and Heart Rate Keep Climbing

If you wear a watch, notice what happens after the first mile or two. On an easy run on flat ground, your effort should settle into something steady. If your breathing and heart rate keep creeping up, you either started too fast or the terrain or weather is making it harder than you realize. Hot, humid days make the same pace feel harder, so adjust by feel.

### Your Legs Feel Beat Up the Next Day

Easy runs shouldn't leave you feeling the way a hard workout does. If your legs feel heavy or sore the morning after a run you meant to keep relaxed, the pace may have been too aggressive. If soreness turns into pain that lingers, changes how you move, or keeps coming back, talk to a doctor or physical therapist.

### You Can't Hold a Conversation

Back to the talk test, because it's that reliable. Group runs are a built-in check: if you're too winded to chat with the person next to you, you're not in easy territory anymore.

## Why Runners Push Too Hard on Easy Days

**Ego and habit.** Former athletes often struggle to run slow. If you spent years training hard, easy pace can feel like a waste of time. It isn't. The aerobic base you build at easy effort supports everything else.

**Trying to keep up.** Running with faster people and feeling like you need to match them is real. The right group won't leave you behind. At [GRYT Run Club](/), the run is no-drop: it ends when the last person finishes, and faster runners loop back, which takes a lot of pressure off pace.

**App pressure.** Watching your pace on a segment or comparing splits to last week can push you harder than you planned. Easy runs don't need to be fast. They need to be easy.

## How to Actually Slow Down

If you've been running easy days too fast for a while, slowing down will feel strange. A few ways to make the adjustment:

### Run by feel, not by pace

Cover your watch face or turn off pace alerts. Use the talk test as your guide. Do this for a few weeks and your sense of effort will recalibrate.

### Start slower than you think

The first mile sets the tone. Start at a pace that feels almost too easy, and only pick it up if the talk test says you have room.

### Take walk breaks

Walking isn't failure. Short walk breaks, especially on hills or in the heat, keep your effort in the easy range and let you cover more total time on your feet.

### Use heart rate as a guide, not a rule

If you train with heart rate, keep easy runs in your lower zones. Heart rate zones vary a lot from person to person, and wrist-based readings aren't always accurate, so treat the number as one input alongside how you feel.

### Run with people who aren't racing

A group with no pace requirement helps here. When nobody around you is chasing a split, the pressure to run fast mostly disappears.

## Easy Doesn't Mean Unproductive

This trips up comeback runners and busy parents with limited time. Easy running feels like it shouldn't count. It does.

Research on how endurance athletes train backs this up. A [review by Stephen Seiler in the International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20861519/) found that successful endurance athletes tend to do about 80 percent of their sessions at low intensity, with about 20 percent dominated by hard work. You're not an elite athlete, but the principle carries over: most of the work is easy, and the hard sessions work better with that base underneath them. Running hard every day makes it harder to recover and stay consistent.

Run easy on your easy days. Run hard on your hard days, if you have them. Keep them separate. If you want a simple structure, GRYT's guide to [building a running base](/resources/build-a-running-base) is a good next step.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a good easy run pace for a recreational runner?**
It varies by person, so use effort instead of a specific number. You should be able to hold a full conversation without pausing for breath.

**How do I know if I'm running too hard on an easy run?**
The clearest signs: you can't finish full sentences, your breathing and heart rate keep climbing, you feel drained afterward, or your legs feel beat up the next morning.

**Should easy runs feel too slow?**
Often, yes, at first. Many runners associate effort with progress. That feeling fades as your aerobic base builds.

**Can I run easy in a group without getting left behind?**
Yes, if the group is no-drop. When the group stays together, the pressure to keep up with faster runners mostly disappears.

**How much of my weekly running should be easy?**
A widely used guideline, based on how endurance athletes train, is roughly 80 percent easy. If you're unsure, erring toward more easy running is rarely the wrong call.

**Does running with a group help or hurt easy pace discipline?**
It depends on the group. A group with no pace requirement and a no-drop format supports easy running. A group where faster runners set the tempo can push everyone harder than intended.

**What's the difference between easy pace and recovery pace?**
People often use the terms interchangeably. Some runners use "recovery" for the very gentlest runs, like the day after a hard workout. Both should pass the talk test comfortably.

Running easy is a skill, and it takes practice. Start with the talk test, be honest about how you feel after each run, and give yourself permission to slow down.
