To find a trail running group near me, search Strava Clubs and Facebook using your city name plus the nearest trail system or neighborhood, check the RRCA Club Finder directory filtered by state, and ask at a specialty running store. Once you have a shortlist, verify each group's typical distance and no-drop policy, message the organizer, and show up five to ten minutes early on your first run.

Trail running continued its decade-long growth in 2024, according to [the Sports & Fitness Industry Association's Topline Participation Report](https://sfia.org/resources/sfias-topline-participation-report-shows-247-1-million-americans-were-active-in-2024/), so there's a good chance a group already meets near you. The steps below give you a practical checklist for finding one that fits.

## Step 1: Search Strava, Facebook, and Meetup

Start with the tools you already use to log miles. Strava Clubs, Facebook Groups, and Meetup all surface active local groups, and each one shows you something slightly different.

**Strava Clubs** lets you search for running clubs by location, view their recent activity, and download route files before you ever show up. You don't need a paid subscription to join a club. You can also judge whether a group is still active by checking how recently members have posted runs. A club with no activity in the past 60 days is probably dormant. Look for one with consistent weekly uploads.

Strava's own data shows why this is a good place to start. Running club participation on the platform rose 59% in 2024, according to [Strava's 2024 Year in Sport report](https://press.strava.com/articles/strava-releases-annual-year-in-sport-trend). Real, active crews are out there. You just need to find the right one.

**Facebook Groups** are where many smaller, hyper-local trail groups live. Search your city or county name plus "trail running" or "trail runners." Filter by groups, then sort by activity. Read the last ten posts before you request to join. You want to see recent run recaps, route photos, and members tagging each other. That signals a group that actually meets.

**Meetup** is worth checking, especially if you live outside a major city where trail-specific groups may not have a Strava presence. Search "trail running" plus your zip code or nearest town. Before you RSVP to anything, check that the group has posted an event within the last 30 days. Meetup groups can go quiet for months and still appear in search results.

When you find a candidate on any platform, note the meeting day, time, and trailhead location. Screenshot it. Confirm the group is still posting before you drive out.

## Step 2: Use National Directories to Find Vetted Trail Running Clubs

Digital platforms show you activity. National directories show you established organizations. The Road Runners Club of America, which calls itself [the oldest and largest national association of running organizations](https://www.rrca.org/about/), lists its member clubs by state.

Go to the [RRCA Find a Running Club page](https://www.rrca.org/clubs/), select your state, and filter by location. Not every result will be trail-specific. Read each club's description and look for words like "trail," "off-road," or named trail systems in your area. If a club lists multiple run types, email or message them directly to ask whether they run trails regularly.

Some road-focused clubs also schedule trail runs, so a club that looks road-oriented on paper may still be what you need.

The RRCA directory works best when you treat it as a starting list, not a final answer. Call or message the clubs that look promising. Ask when they last ran trails and how many people typically show up. A club that answers quickly and gives you a specific number is a club that is actually meeting.

## Step 3: Check for Specialty Groups That Match Your Background

Not every trail running group is built the same way. Some skew competitive. Others are explicitly social. A growing number are designed for runners who want a crew that reflects their identity.

Many people showing up to trail groups for the first time are road runners making a shift, not lifelong mountain runners. Groups built for that transition exist, and they are worth finding.

If you want a women-focused group, check [Trail Sisters Local Groups](https://trailsisters.net/local-groups/). Trail Sisters runs a network of free, volunteer-led local groups that are no-drop and welcome all paces, and its directory lists groups by state, including one in Pittsburgh.

For BIPOC runners, search specifically for "Black Runners," "Brown Girls Do Run," or similar named groups in your metro area. These communities exist in most mid-size and large cities and are often more active on Instagram than on Strava or Meetup.

Beginner-focused groups are easy to spot. Look for phrases like "all paces welcome," "no runner left behind," or "social run" in a group's description. Those phrases signal a conversational pace and a no-drop approach on the trail.

## Step 4: Ask at a Local Specialty Running Store

No app surfaces every group. Specialty running stores fill that gap.

Staff at a specialty store run locally. They know which groups meet on which trails, which ones are beginner-friendly, and which ones quietly folded six months ago but still show up in Google results. That knowledge does not live online. It lives behind the counter.

Walk in and ask directly: "Are there any trail running groups that meet near here?" A good running store will name two or three, tell you the meeting day and time, and often know the organizer personally. Some stores host their own group runs or post a bulletin board with flyers for local clubs. Check the board near the door or the register.

Ask about the post-run coffee stop too. Some trail groups end at a diner or coffee shop near the trailhead. A crew that grabs coffee together after a Saturday run is a different experience than one that disperses the moment the run ends, and knowing that detail in advance tells you something real about the group's culture.

If the store staff run trails themselves, ask which group they run with. A personal recommendation from someone who has shown up to the same run is more useful than any directory listing.

Visit on a weekday morning or early Saturday, when staff who run are most likely to be working. Come with a specific question: your pace range, your preferred distance, and the days you are free. The more specific you are, the more useful the answer.

## How to Vet a Trail Running Group Before You Show Up

Finding a group is step one. Making sure it fits is step two. Before you drive to a trailhead, run through this checklist.

**1. Confirm the typical distance.**
Distances vary a lot by group. Some go long. Check the group's recent posts or route files and make sure the distance matches where you are right now, not where you want to be in three months.

**2. Ask about the no-drop policy.**
No-drop means no one gets left behind on the trail. The group waits at junctions, loops back for slower runners, or pairs faster runners with those who need support. This policy is common but not universal. Ask directly before you show up. A group that cannot answer the question clearly probably does not have a formal policy.

**3. Check the pace range.**
"All paces welcome" means different things to different groups. Look at recent run recaps and note the times. If a group posts 8-minute miles on technical terrain and calls that "casual," that is useful information. Many groups run at a social pace, but verify it.

**4. Read the last ten posts.**
Active groups post run recaps, route photos, and upcoming events regularly. If the last post is from two months ago, the group may have gone quiet. Look for members tagging each other and asking questions. That back-and-forth signals a real community.

**5. Check the meeting location.**
Confirm the exact trailhead, not just the trail name. Many trails have multiple access points spread miles apart. A wrong parking lot means you miss the group entirely. Screenshot the address and drop a pin before you leave home.

**6. Look for safety expectations.**
Does the group mention carrying water, wearing a GPS watch, or having a charged phone? Groups that talk openly about trail safety are groups that have thought through what happens when something goes wrong. That matters more on a wooded trail than on a road loop through a neighborhood.

**7. Note the post-run plan.**
A group that meets for coffee after the run is signaling that the social connection matters as much as the miles. That is worth knowing before you show up, especially if you are new and want time to meet people without the pressure of keeping pace.

## Step 5: Message the Organizer and Prepare for Your First Run

Once you have picked a group, reach out before you show up. A short message does two things: it confirms the run is still happening, and it tells the organizer to expect a new face.

Keep the message simple. Introduce yourself, mention your approximate pace or experience level, and confirm the meeting location and start time. Something like: "Hi, I found your group on Strava. I'm a road runner moving to trails, comfortable around a 10-minute mile. Planning to join this Saturday at the main trailhead lot. Is that still the right spot?" That is enough.

Many organizers respond quickly and appreciate the heads-up. If no one replies within 48 hours, send a follow-up or check the group's most recent post for schedule changes.

On the day of the run, follow these steps in order:

1. Arrive 5 to 10 minutes early. The group gathers before it departs, and showing up at the exact start time means you miss introductions.
2. Introduce yourself to the organizer or the first person you see. Say it is your first time.
3. Bring water, even for a short run. Trail terrain takes more out of you than road miles at the same distance.
4. Tell someone your experience level before you start. A good group will adjust accordingly.
5. Stay with the group. If you feel strong, resist the urge to push ahead on your first run. Learn the trail and the crew first.

## Is Trail Running Good for Beginners Looking for a Group?

Yes, as long as you pick the right group. Many trail groups run at a social pace and welcome beginners. The person next to you on a group trail run may well have started on roads too. Start with a beginner-friendly, no-drop group on easier terrain, and build up to technical trails over time.

If you'd rather start with a smooth surface before you head into the woods, a rail trail group is a gentle on-ramp. In the South Hills of Pittsburgh, for example, GRYT Run Club meets every Saturday at 7:00 AM at 212 Valley Brook Road in McMurray, PA, on the Montour/Peters Trail. It's a flat, paved rail trail, not technical singletrack. Walkers, joggers, and runners cover about 4 miles out and back (you can turn around early), then grab coffee in the parking lot. It's free and no-drop, and first-timers [RSVP online](/) and sign a short waiver.

Say it is your first time when you arrive. A good group will make sure someone who knows the trail stays with you.

## FAQs

### Do people pay to join trail running clubs?

Most trail running clubs are free to join and free to attend. Some larger clubs charge a small annual membership fee to cover insurance or event costs, but the weekly group run itself rarely costs anything. If a group asks for payment before you have attended a single run, ask what the fee covers before you commit.

### What is a no-drop policy in trail running?

A no-drop policy means no one gets left behind on the trail. Faster runners wait at trail junctions, loop back for slower runners, or pair up with anyone who needs support. It matters on trails because the terrain is less predictable than roads and finding your way alone can be hard for someone new to a trail system. Before joining any group, ask directly whether they have a no-drop policy. A group that can give you a clear answer has thought about it. One that cannot probably has not.

### How do I find the best trail running group near me if I live outside a major city?

Start with Meetup, which hosts trail-specific groups in smaller towns that may not have a Strava presence. Then check the RRCA Club Finder for established clubs in your state, and ask at the nearest specialty running store. Staff know which groups are still active and which ones folded months ago but still show up in search results. A personal recommendation from someone who runs locally is worth more than any directory listing.

### What should I wear or bring to my first group trail run?

Wear trail shoes if you have them, but road shoes are usually fine on smooth, beginner-friendly trails. For clothing by weather, see [what to wear running](/resources/what-to-wear-running). Bring water even if the run is short. Trail terrain demands more effort per mile than road running, and you will want it. Carry your phone with GPS turned on. Tell someone at the trailhead your experience level before the run starts. A good group will adjust. Skip the headphones on your first run so you can hear the group and the trail.

### How far do most trail running groups typically run together?

It varies widely. Many social groups stay in a moderate range, while some weekend groups go much longer, particularly those training for races. Before you show up, check the group's recent route files or post history to confirm the typical distance matches where you are right now. A group running 12 miles on technical terrain is not the right starting point if you are new to trails.

## Conclusion

Search Strava Clubs, Facebook, and Meetup first. Cross-check with the RRCA Club Finder for established clubs. Ask at a specialty running store for groups that never made it online. Then confirm the distance, the no-drop policy, and the pace range before you commit.

Once you have a group that looks like a fit, message the organizer, arrive five to ten minutes early, and introduce yourself before the run starts. The miles are straightforward. The five minutes before they begin are where you actually join the crew.
