You signed up for a 10K. Maybe a friend talked you into it, maybe it's a spring race you've circled on the calendar, or maybe it's just a goal you set late one night when you felt ambitious. Either way, you're here, and you want a plan that actually works.

Eight weeks is enough time. You don't need to be fast. You don't need to have run recently. You need a structure you'll stick to and enough consistency to reach the start line feeling ready.

This plan is built for nervous beginners and comeback runners: people who used to run and stopped, or who have never gone farther than a mile but want to change that. No pace requirements. No jargon. Just a week-by-week framework that gets you to 6.2 miles.

If you have a heart condition, another health issue, or a past injury, or you haven't been active in a long time, check with your doctor before starting. If you're starting from zero, a few weeks of regular walking first is a smart warm-up for this plan.

## What to Expect Over 8 Weeks

The plan follows a simple arc: build your base in weeks one through four, extend your long run in weeks five and six, then ease back in weeks seven and eight so you arrive at race day with fresh legs.

Three runs per week, with optional walks or cross-training, is plenty. Rest days matter as much as run days, because that's when your body adapts. One long run per week is the anchor. Everything else supports it.

## The 8-Week Plan, Week by Week

### Week 1: Just Start Moving

Your goal this week isn't to impress yourself. It's to establish the habit.

- **Day 1:** 20-minute easy run/walk. Run 2 minutes, walk 1 minute. Repeat.
- **Day 2:** Rest or a 20-minute walk.
- **Day 3:** 25-minute easy run/walk. Same intervals.
- **Day 4:** Rest.
- **Day 5:** 30-minute long run/walk. Push the run intervals to 3 minutes if it feels comfortable.

Easy means conversational. If you can't say a full sentence out loud, slow down.

### Week 2: Find a Rhythm

- **Day 1:** 25-minute run. Run 3 minutes, walk 1 minute.
- **Day 2:** Rest or cross-train (bike, swim, walk).
- **Day 3:** 30-minute run. Same intervals.
- **Day 4:** Rest.
- **Day 5:** 35-minute long run. Try running 5 minutes, walking 1 minute.

By the end of this week, the run intervals should feel manageable, not heroic.

### Week 3: Extend the Run Intervals

- **Day 1:** 30-minute run. Run 6 minutes, walk 1 minute.
- **Day 2:** Rest.
- **Day 3:** 30-minute run. Same.
- **Day 4:** Rest or easy walk.
- **Day 5:** 40-minute long run. Run 8 minutes, walk 1 minute.

If the 8-minute intervals feel hard, stay at 6. There's no prize for rushing.

### Week 4: First Continuous Miles

This is where things shift. You're going to try running without planned walk breaks. If that's too big a jump, repeat week 3 first. There's no penalty for taking nine or ten weeks.

- **Day 1:** 30-minute easy run. Walk only if you need to.
- **Day 2:** Rest.
- **Day 3:** 30-minute run at a comfortable pace.
- **Day 4:** Rest.
- **Day 5:** Long run, aiming for about 4 miles. Walk breaks are fine. Finishing is the goal.

Four miles is about what GRYT Run Club covers every Saturday morning on the Montour/Peters Trail in McMurray. If you're in the South Hills and want to run that distance with people around you, that's what the club does every week.

### Week 5: Build Toward 5 Miles

- **Day 1:** 35-minute easy run.
- **Day 2:** Rest or cross-train.
- **Day 3:** 35-minute run, slightly harder effort through the middle 15 minutes.
- **Day 4:** Rest.
- **Day 5:** Long run of 5 miles. Take it slow. This is your longest run to date.

Five miles will feel long. That's normal. You're supposed to feel a little tired at the end.

### Week 6: Peak Week

This is the hardest week of the plan. You're going to run your longest distance before race day.

- **Day 1:** 35-minute easy run.
- **Day 2:** Rest.
- **Day 3:** 40-minute run. Include 10 minutes at a comfortably hard effort.
- **Day 4:** Rest.
- **Day 5:** Long run of 5.5 to 6 miles. Walk breaks allowed. Just cover the distance.

After this run, you've already proven to yourself that you can do it. The 10K is 6.2 miles. You're basically there.

### Week 7: Back Off and Recover

Resist the urge to keep pushing. Your body needs this week to absorb the work.

- **Day 1:** 30-minute easy run.
- **Day 2:** Rest.
- **Day 3:** 25-minute easy run.
- **Day 4:** Rest.
- **Day 5:** 4-mile easy run. Comfortable pace, no pressure.

By the end of this week you might feel a little antsy. Good. That's your body telling you it's ready.

### Week 8: Race Week

Keep moving, but don't overdo it.

- **Day 1:** 20-minute easy run or jog.
- **Day 2:** Rest.
- **Day 3:** 15-minute shakeout run. Easy and loose, just waking up the legs.
- **Day 4:** Rest. Lay out your gear. Eat a normal dinner.
- **Race Day:** Run your 10K.

## Gear You Actually Need

Not much. A pair of running shoes that fit properly is the one non-negotiable. They don't need to be expensive, but they should be running-specific and not worn down.

For early morning, evening, or any low-light training, bring a headlamp or clip-on light and wear something reflective, especially near roads.

Everything else (a GPS watch, compression socks, a hydration vest) is optional. Start simple.

## How to Handle the Hard Days

Some runs will feel bad. Your legs will be heavy, your breathing will be off, and you'll want to stop. That's not a sign you're failing. It's a normal part of training.

On those days, slow down. Walk if you need to. Finish the time, not necessarily the distance. Showing up and moving is the work.

There's a difference between tired and hurt, though. Sharp pain, pain that changes how you run, or pain that's still there the next day is a reason to stop and rest, and to see a doctor or physical therapist if it doesn't improve.

## What to Do on Race Day

Wake up early enough to eat something light and familiar before the start, like toast, a banana, or oatmeal. Many runners like one to three hours to digest. Nothing new.

Arrive early. Walk around. Stay warm. Don't sprint the first mile just because you feel good. The first mile always feels good. Save something for miles four and five.

When it gets hard in the final stretch, remember that you've already covered this distance in training. Your legs know what to do.

## After the Race

Take a few easy days. Walk, stretch, sleep. Your body did something real and it needs time to recover.

Then decide what's next. A lot of people cross their first 10K finish line and immediately want to sign up for another race. That's a good sign. Channel it into a plan, not just a registration. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a complete beginner finish a 10K in 8 weeks?**
Yes, with consistent effort. This plan is designed specifically for people who are new to running or returning after a long break. Walk breaks are built in, and weekly mileage increases gradually so your body has time to adapt.

**How many days per week do I need to run?**
Three to four days is enough. More isn't always better, especially early on. Rest days are when your body actually gets stronger.

**What if I miss a week?**
Don't try to make it up. Pick up where you left off, or repeat the week you missed. Skipping ahead after a break raises your injury risk. Consistency over time matters more than any single week.

**Do I need to run the full 6.2 miles before race day?**
No. This plan peaks at 5.5 to 6 miles in week 6, which is close enough. Race-day adrenaline and fresh legs from the lighter final weeks usually cover the rest.

**What pace should I run?**
Easy pace means you can hold a conversation. If you're breathing too hard to talk, slow down. For long runs especially, slower is almost always better. The only harder efforts in this plan are short sections of the midweek runs in weeks five and six.

**What should I eat before a long run?**
Something light and familiar, at least an hour before you head out. Toast with peanut butter, a banana, or oatmeal all work well. Avoid anything heavy, greasy, or new. Race day is not the time to experiment with food.

**Is it okay to run with a group during this training plan?**
Yes. Running with others makes the miles easier and keeps you accountable. If you're in Pittsburgh's South Hills, GRYT Run Club meets every Saturday at 7:00 AM at no cost and welcomes all paces. That Saturday run lines up well with the long run days in this plan.

Eight weeks from now, you could be at a finish line. The work is straightforward: show up, follow the plan, and don't skip the rest days.
