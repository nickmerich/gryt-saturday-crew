Finding a slow runners club near you is easier than most people expect. Groups that welcome walkers, joggers, and run-walkers exist in most cities and many suburbs. Look for "no-drop" or "all paces welcome" language, check local running stores and club directories, and show up once. Most groups are far less intimidating than they look from the outside.

## What Do "No-Drop" and "All Paces Welcome" Mean?

Both terms mean the group shouldn't leave you behind. "No-drop" is the stronger of the two. Clubs that use it wait at turns, have faster runners loop back, or assign a sweep runner to stay with the last person. Nobody finishes alone. "All paces welcome" says the club doesn't filter by speed, but it doesn't always come with the same structure.

The difference matters most on longer routes. A club that says "all paces welcome" but runs one big group with no plan for the back may still leave slower runners finishing alone. A real no-drop club has a plan for keeping everyone connected.

When you're scanning club listings, treat "no-drop" as a green flag. If you don't see it, ask. A club worth joining will answer without hesitation.

## Where to Find a Slow Runners Club Near You

- **Local running stores.** Many specialty running stores host free weekly group runs, and staff know which local groups welcome slower paces. Walk in and ask.
- **RRCA club finder.** The Road Runners Club of America has a [club finder organized by state](https://www.rrca.org/clubs/). Some member clubs list run-walk programs or beginner groups.
- **parkrun.** Free, timed Saturday 5Ks with no time limit. [parkrun USA lists more than 100 events](https://www.parkrun.us/), and every event has a volunteer tail walker who finishes last.
- **Meetup and Facebook groups.** Search "[your city] run club," "beginner runners," or "run walk group." Many informal groups live only on Facebook.
- **Strava clubs.** Search local clubs by location and look at recent group runs to see whether paces actually vary.
- **Identity-based groups.** Groups like [Black Girls RUN!](https://blackgirlsrun.com/find-a-community/), which lists free local groups nationwide, often welcome every pace.

## Do Running Clubs Have Pace Requirements?

Most social run clubs don't. The clubs that do are usually competitive training groups, track clubs, or race teams built around structured speed work. If a club's homepage leads with race results and qualifying standards, it's built for performance, not participation.

Some clubs organize around a specific goal, like a first half marathon or trail racing. A goal-focused club can still be no-drop. Ask the organizer what most members are training for and how they handle different paces.

The clearest sign of an unwritten pace requirement is how a club talks. If every post is about PRs and podiums, slower runners will feel that pressure even without a written rule. Look for groups that post photos of everyone finishing, not just the front of the pack.

## How to Join a Running Club as a Slow Runner

1. **Find two or three clubs that use no-drop or all-paces language.** Check their social media for photos of actual group runs.
2. **Reach out before you show up.** Message the organizer or post in the club's group: "I'm a slower runner and take walk breaks. Is this a good fit?" A welcoming group answers quickly and specifically.
3. **Have a rough plan.** You don't need a training program to join, but knowing roughly how far you can comfortably go helps you pace day one. If you want a starting point, the [free run plan builder](/plan) pairs well with a weekly group run.
4. **Bring someone.** A friend or neighbor, even one who doesn't run much, makes the first visit less awkward.
5. **Arrive early.** Get there five to ten minutes before the start. Tell the organizer you're new and ask about the plan for the day.
6. **Run your own pace.** Start at the back or the middle and run comfortably. Notice how the group handles slower runners.
7. **Go back a second time before you decide.** The first run is always a little awkward. The second one tells you whether the group fits.

## Are Walk-Run Intervals Welcome in a Group?

They should be. Alternating running and walking is a well-established way to build endurance and manage effort. Olympian and coach Jeff Galloway has taught his [Run Walk Run method](https://www.jeffgalloway.com/training/run-walk/) to beginners since the 1970s, and many training groups use it for races up to the marathon.

The practical question is whether the club's format supports it. A no-drop group has no problem with walk breaks because it already plans for different paces. If the group runs as a single pack, ask the organizer how they handle walk breaks. If the answer is dismissive, that group isn't really all-paces, whatever its description says.

It helps to tell the people near you what you're doing: "I take walk breaks every few minutes." Then nobody is confused when you slow down.

If walking or running causes pain that doesn't go away, check with a doctor or physical therapist before pushing through it.

## What If There's No Slow Runners Club Near You?

Start one. It's less complicated than it sounds.

1. **Pick one place and one time, and keep both.** A trailhead, park entrance, or coffee shop near a path. Choose somewhere with parking and a clear starting point.
2. **Post it publicly and keep posting.** Create a free Meetup event or Facebook group with one clear sentence: "Slow, no-drop group run, walkers welcome, [place], [time], every week." Post it every week, even if only two people show up.
3. **Set the tone from the first run.** Wait for everyone. Loop back for anyone behind. Hang around and talk after.
4. **Let it grow slowly.** Three people who show up every week beat thirty who show up once.

## FAQs

### What is a no-drop run?

A no-drop run means the group doesn't leave anyone behind. Some clubs loop back for slower runners, some assign a sweep runner, and some stay together as one pack. Ask how a club handles it in practice. A good answer is specific.

### Are there running clubs for beginners near me?

Most social run clubs accept beginners even if they don't say so. Search Meetup, Facebook, Strava, and the RRCA club finder using terms like "beginner runners," "all paces," or "run walk." Local running stores are another good source.

### How far do most running clubs go?

Many social runs cover roughly 3 to 5 miles, and many offer shorter options. Check a club's recent posts or ask the organizer before your first visit.

### What should I wear or bring to my first run club meetup?

Comfortable shoes, weather-appropriate layers, and water if it's warm or the route is long. You don't need a GPS watch or special gear. The [what to wear running guide](/resources/what-to-wear-running) covers cold-weather layers.

### Can I join a running club if I mostly walk?

Yes, if the club says it's no-drop or walkers welcome. If you're unsure, message the organizer first. The answer will tell you whether the inclusive language is real.

## Conclusion

Slow runners clubs exist in most places, and the good ones don't care about your pace. They care that you show up. Look for no-drop and all-paces groups through running stores, directories, and social media, check that the culture matches the description, and give a group two visits before you decide.

If nothing local fits, pick a time and a place and post it. Three people on a Saturday morning is a run club.
