Pittsburgh doesn't ease you in. Step out your door in plenty of neighborhoods and you're on a hill within a block. If you've ever laced up in the South Hills and felt your lungs protest by the second block, you're not alone. The terrain here is hard, and it rewards runners who learn to work with it rather than fight it.

Whether you're training for the 2027 DICK'S Sporting Goods Pittsburgh Marathon, building toward next year's Great Race, or just running around your neighborhood, these hill running tips will help you stop dreading the climbs and start using them.

## Why Pittsburgh Hills Are Different

Most running advice about hills assumes you'll run into them now and then. In much of the Pittsburgh area, they're hard to avoid. Many South Hills streets have short, steep pitches that call for a different approach than the long, gradual grades you'd find somewhere flatter.

Not every route is hilly, though. The Montour/Peters Trail in McMurray is a flat, paved rail trail, which makes it a great place for easy miles and recovery runs between hill days. For the climbs themselves, you'll want neighborhood streets or parks with real grades.

Once you know that many local hills are short and punchy, the way you prepare for them changes.

## Hill Running Tips That Actually Work

### Shorten Your Stride on the Way Up

The most common mistake on a steep climb is trying to hold your flat-ground stride. Shorten your stride, keep your feet landing under your hips, and lean slightly forward from the ankles, not the waist.

Think of quick, light steps rather than big pushes. Keeping your cadence steady while your stride shortens can help keep your effort from spiking.

### Use Effort, Not Pace, as Your Gauge

Your GPS watch can't tell you how hard a hill is. A 10-minute mile on flat ground and a 10-minute mile up a steep street are very different efforts. Chase pace on a climb and you'll run out of gas before the top.

Run by feel instead. A moderate hill should feel harder than flat running, but you should still be able to get a few words out. If you're gasping, back off. The goal is to reach the top with something left.

### Don't Neglect the Downhill

Runners put so much energy into getting up the hill that they forget about coming down. That quad soreness the day after a hilly run often comes from the descents, because your muscles work hard to control your speed on the way down.

On the way down, resist the urge to let gravity take over completely. Keep your knees soft, stay tall or lean very slightly forward, and take quick, light steps. Heavy braking on every step adds strain to your quads and knees. Practice controlled downhills regularly, especially if you have a downhill-heavy race coming up.

### Build Hill Strength Gradually

If you're coming back after a break, or you're new to running hills, don't start with the steepest street you can find. Start with a gentle grade and a few short climbs mixed into an easy run.

Once moderate grades feel manageable, try one hill repeat session per week. Find a hill that takes about 60 to 90 seconds to climb, run up at a hard but controlled effort, walk or jog back down, and repeat four to six times. That's enough to build strength without wrecking your legs for the rest of the week. Do your other runs at an easy effort, including flat ones like the rail trail.

### Strengthen What the Hills Demand

Hill running asks a lot of your glutes, calves, quads, and hips. Single-leg exercises, calf raises, step-ups, and hip hinge movements like Romanian deadlifts are all worth adding. You don't need a gym. Bodyweight versions done consistently can make a noticeable difference over time. See [cross-training for runners](/resources/cross-training-for-runners) for more ideas.

### Breathe Intentionally

On a steep climb, breathing tends to get shallow and fast. Some runners find it helps to breathe rhythmically, matching breaths to steps, such as three steps in and two steps out at moderate effort, and a quicker pattern on harder climbs. It feels mechanical at first. If it doesn't work for you, just focus on steady, relaxed breathing.

### Walk When You Need To

There's no shame in walking a steep section, especially on a longer run. Walking a short pitch and reaching the top with your effort under control is often smarter than grinding up and spending the next half mile recovering.

## Putting It Into Practice Locally

A simple week for South Hills runners might look like this:

- **One hill day:** Short repeats on a nearby neighborhood hill, or an easy run on a hilly route.
- **Easy days:** Flat miles, like the Montour/Peters Trail, at a conversational pace.
- **Strength:** Two short sessions of lower-body strength work.

Running with others helps too. Climbs feel more manageable with company, and easy group miles keep you consistent between harder days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I stop getting so winded on Pittsburgh hills?**
Shorten your stride, lean slightly forward from the ankles, and run by effort rather than pace. Most runners get winded because they try to hold their flat-ground speed on a climb. Slow down, breathe steadily, and give your body several weeks of regular hill running to adapt.

**Is it better to run up hills slowly or walk them?**
It depends on the grade and your fitness. On moderate hills, slow running builds strength and fitness. On very steep pitches, especially during a long run, walking is often the smarter call. Both are valid.

**How often should I do hill training?**
For most runners, one hill-focused session per week is a good start, with easy runs on other days. Add more only if you're recovering well.

**What muscles do I need to strengthen for hill running?**
Glutes, calves, and hips work hard on climbs, and quads absorb much of the load on descents. Single-leg exercises, calf raises, step-ups, and hip hinges are practical additions.

**Is the Montour/Peters Trail good for hill training?**
Not really. It's a flat, paved rail trail, which makes it better for easy miles and recovery runs. For hills, use nearby neighborhood streets or parks with steady grades, and save the trail for your easy days.

**Do I need special shoes for hill running in Pittsburgh?**
For roads and paved paths, a standard road running shoe works fine. If you're running on dirt trails, wet grass, or muddy hills, a trail shoe with more grip is worth considering.

**How do I train for the Pittsburgh Marathon hills specifically?**
Add a weekly hill session on a 60- to 90-second climb, include rolling terrain in some long runs, and practice controlled downhills. Keep your easy days easy so you recover between hill workouts.

The hills aren't going anywhere. Start with the fundamentals, be patient, and find people to run with. It gets easier when you're not doing it alone.
