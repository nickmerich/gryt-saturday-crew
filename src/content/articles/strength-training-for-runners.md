Most runners skip strength work. Not because they don't believe in it, but because every article they find assumes a gym membership, an hour to spare, and some background in lifting. None of that fits the average person just trying to stay consistent with their running.

Strength training for runners doesn't need to be complicated. It needs to be specific. The six moves below target areas runners commonly need help with: glutes, hips, inner thighs, single-leg control, and calves. You can do all of them at home with your bodyweight, a sturdy chair, and an optional resistance band.

## Why Runners Need Strength Work

Running is repetitive. Every mile is well over a thousand steps, loading the same muscles, tendons, and joints in the same pattern. That repetition builds aerobic fitness. Strength work builds the capacity to handle it.

The evidence for strength training and injury is encouraging. [A 2014 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK169555/), covering 25 trials and more than 26,000 participants across sports, found that strength training programs cut sports injuries to less than a third, while stretching showed no clear benefit. Those trials weren't all in runners, but it's a strong reason to make room for strength work.

It doesn't replace running. It helps make running sustainable.

## The 6 Moves That Actually Matter

Each of these addresses an area that often comes up for runners at every pace and mileage level. If any exercise causes pain (not just effort), stop and check with a physical therapist.

### 1. Single-Leg Glute Bridge

Lie on your back, one foot flat on the floor, the other leg extended. Drive through the planted heel, lift your hips until your body forms a straight line from shoulder to knee, then lower slowly.

This is a simple, low-impact glute exercise. The single-leg version also shows you any side-to-side difference right away. If one side shakes and the other doesn't, you've found something to work on.

**Start here:** 3 sets of 10 per side.

### 2. Lateral Band Walk (or Bodyweight Side Step)

With a resistance band just above the knees (or without one if you're starting out), stand with feet hip-width apart, knees slightly bent, and step sideways. Keep your hips level. Don't let the trailing foot drag.

This targets the gluteus medius, the muscle on the side of your hip that helps keep your pelvis level when you're on one leg, which is every step of running. Hip strength comes up often in runner injury research; for example, [a 2000 Stanford study of distance runners](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/hip-abductor-weakness-in-distance-runners-with-iliotibial-band-sy/) found weaker hip abductors on the injured side in runners with IT band syndrome.

**Start here:** 3 sets of 15 steps each direction.

### 3. Reverse Lunge

Step backward with one foot, lower your back knee toward the floor, then drive through your front foot to return. That's one rep.

Many people find reverse lunges easier on the knees than forward lunges because you're not braking a forward step. They build single-leg strength and control, which helps on hills and downhill stretches.

**Start here:** 3 sets of 8 per side.

### 4. Copenhagen Plank

Lie on your side, prop yourself up on your forearm, and rest your top leg on a chair or bench (at the knee to make it easier, at the ankle to make it harder). Lift your hips and hold, keeping your bottom leg off the floor if you can.

This one looks strange and earns its place. It targets the adductors, the inner thigh muscles that many runner programs ignore. In [a 2019 randomized trial in the British Journal of Sports Medicine](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/53/3/150.full.pdf), soccer players who did a simple Copenhagen adduction program had a 41% lower risk of groin problems. That was soccer, not running, but it's one of the best-studied single exercises for the inner thigh.

**Start here:** 3 sets of 15 to 20 seconds per side, using the easier knee-supported version. Build gradually.

### 5. Step-Up with Knee Drive

Stand in front of a sturdy chair or low step. Step up with one foot, drive the opposite knee up toward your chest at the top, then step back down with control.

This is about as close to running mechanics as a strength exercise gets: you load one leg, extend through the hip, and drive the opposite knee. It also builds the quad and glute strength you use on hills.

**Start here:** 3 sets of 10 per side. Use a lower step if your knees complain.

### 6. Single-Leg Calf Raise

Stand on one foot (hold a wall for balance), rise onto your toes as high as you can, then lower slowly. That's it.

Your calves and Achilles do a lot of work on every stride, and calf and Achilles complaints are common among runners. Slow, controlled calf raises, especially the lowering phase, are a simple way to build strength there. Doing them off the edge of a step adds range once the flat version feels easy.

**Start here:** 3 sets of 15 per side. Add a backpack with a little weight when this gets easy.

## How to Fit This into a Running Week

You don't need a dedicated gym day. About 20 minutes twice a week is a practical target.

The simplest approach: do this routine on two days that aren't your hardest running days. If you run Saturday, Tuesday, and Thursday, Monday and Wednesday work well. These moves are low-impact, but expect some soreness in the first week or two, so keep the volume modest at first.

If you're building back from a break, start with one set of each exercise and add a set each week. One set done consistently beats three sets done twice and then abandoned.

## What These Moves Won't Do

They won't make you faster on their own. Strength work is a foundation, not a shortcut. It helps you stay healthy enough to keep training, which is how it pays off over time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a gym to do strength training as a runner?**
No. Every exercise here uses bodyweight or an optional resistance band. A sturdy chair handles the step-ups and Copenhagen plank. The full routine works in your living room or driveway.

**How often should runners do strength training?**
Twice a week is a practical target for most runners. Once a week is better than nothing. Consistency over months matters more than any single week.

**Will strength training make me slower or heavier?**
Not with this kind of routine. These are low-volume, bodyweight exercises aimed at strength and control, not building a lot of muscle mass.

**When in my training week should I do strength work?**
Avoid a hard strength session the day before a hard run or long run. Easy days and rest days are the best fit.

**I've had knee pain before. Are any of these exercises safe?**
Many runners with a history of knee pain tolerate glute bridges, side steps, and calf raises well, and can adjust lunges and step-ups with a shorter range or lower step. If you have a current injury, ongoing pain, or recent surgery, check with a doctor or physical therapist before adding new exercises.

**Can I do this routine if I'm just getting back into running?**
Yes. Comeback runners often have the fitness to push harder than their legs are ready for. Starting strength work early, with one set of each move, helps build capacity as your mileage climbs. The [build a running base guide](/resources/build-a-running-base) covers the running side.

**How long before I notice a difference?**
Many people notice they feel stronger and more stable within several weeks of consistent work. Injury benefits are harder to feel, because they show up as the runs you don't miss.

Consistency compounds. Show up for the strength work the same way you show up for the miles, and the miles get easier.
