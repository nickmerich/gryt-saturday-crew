Most people who quit running in the first month don't quit because they're not athletic enough. They quit because nobody told them how it actually works.

These 12 tips are the basics experienced runners wish someone had told them before the first run, not after the first sore week. Whether you're starting from scratch, coming back after a long gap, or finally following through on something you've been putting off, these are worth knowing before you lace up.

## 1. Slow Down More Than You Think You Need To

This is the one most beginners ignore, and it matters most. If you can't hold a conversation while running, you're going too fast for an easy run. The [CDC's talk test](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/measuring/index.html) is a simple rule of thumb: at moderate effort you can talk but not sing, and at vigorous effort you can only get out a few words before pausing for a breath.

Running too hard too soon is a fast route to burnout and the quiet conclusion that you're "not a runner." Slow down until it feels almost embarrassingly easy. That pace is probably right.

## 2. Walk Breaks Are Not Failure

Run-walk intervals are a normal way to build a base. Running two minutes, walking one, and repeating that for 30 minutes is training.

In the early weeks, the goal is time on your feet, not unbroken mileage. Give yourself permission to walk.

## 3. Pick Shoes That Feel Comfortable

You don't need a fancy gait analysis to start. A large [2014 study of 927 novice runners in the British Journal of Sports Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092202) found that moderate foot pronation was not linked to higher injury risk in runners wearing a neutral shoe. Researchers like [Benno Nigg have proposed](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-095054) that comfort is a sensible way to choose, though that idea is still being tested.

A running specialty store is still a good place to try on several pairs and jog in them. Pick the one that feels best on your feet. Almost everything else about starting to run is free.

## 4. Three Runs Per Week Is Enough

New runners tend to do too much too soon. Three runs per week, with rest days in between, gives your body time to adapt. Muscles, tendons, and bones adapt more slowly than your heart and lungs, so you can feel ready for more before your legs actually are.

Build the habit before you build the mileage.

## 5. Build Gradually (The 10 Percent Rule Is a Guideline, Not Magic)

You'll often hear "don't increase weekly mileage by more than 10 percent." It's a reasonable, conservative guideline, but the research behind it is thin. A [randomized trial of 532 novice runners](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/no-effect-of-a-graded-training-program-on-the-number-of-running-r/) found injury rates were about the same (20.8% vs. 20.3%) for a slower 10-percent-based program and a standard 8-week program.

The takeaway: increase slowly, pay attention to how your body responds, and back off when something hurts. If pain sticks around or changes how you move, talk to a doctor or physical therapist.

## 6. Get Outside When You Can

Treadmills are convenient and perfectly good for training, especially in bad weather. But running outside gets you used to real terrain, wind, and the mental work of covering actual ground. If you have access to a trail or a safe route, use it. The variety helps keep you engaged.

## 7. Consistency Beats Intensity

One hard run a week surrounded by days of nothing does less for you than three easy runs spread through the week. Your body adapts to regular stress, not occasional stress.

Show up consistently, even when the run is short.

## 8. What You Do After the Run Matters

Drink some water. Eat a normal meal or snack with some protein and carbs. Do a few gentle stretches if they feel good. This is basic maintenance that helps you feel ready to run again in a couple of days.

## 10. Dress Like It's Warmer Than It Is

You warm up fast once you're moving. A common rule of thumb is to dress for 10 to 20 degrees warmer than the actual temperature. A light layer you can tie around your waist beats a heavy jacket you can't take off. Our [what to wear running guide](/resources/what-to-wear-running) goes deeper.

If you run in the dark, add reflective gear and a light so drivers can see you.

## 11. Track Your Runs, But Don't Obsess Over the Numbers

A simple log, whether that's Strava or a notebook, helps you see progress over time. That matters in the early weeks when improvement is hard to feel.

Just don't let pace data become a source of anxiety. Your easy runs should feel easy. If every run turns into a race against your last one, you're missing the point.

## 12. The Hardest Part Is the Decision to Start

Not the first mile. Not the first hill. The hardest part is putting on your shoes and walking out the door when you're tired, when it's cold, when you haven't run in months.

That friction is real, and every runner feels it. The ones who stick with it have mostly learned that the decision is the only thing standing between them and the run.

You don't need a perfect week. You need shoes and a decision.

## One More Thing: Find Your People Early

Exercising with others can do more than keep you accountable. In a [2017 study in the Journal of the American Osteopathic Association](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29084328/), medical students who did weekly group fitness classes reported lower stress and better quality of life after 12 weeks, while students who exercised on their own mostly didn't see the same changes. It was a small study, but it matches what a lot of runners notice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many days a week should a beginner run?**
Three days per week is a solid starting point. It's enough to build the habit and improve fitness while leaving recovery time between sessions.

**Is it okay to walk during a run?**
Yes. Run-walk intervals are a legitimate training method for beginners and experienced runners alike.

**How do I know if I'm running too fast?**
Use the talk test. If you can't speak a full sentence without gasping, slow down. Most beginners run their easy days too hard.

**What should I wear for a run in the dark?**
Something reflective and a light source. A headlamp or clip-on light, a reflective vest, and bright clothing all help drivers and cyclists see you.

**How long does it take to feel comfortable running?**
It varies, but many beginners notice running feels easier after a few weeks of consistent, easy running. The first couple of weeks are often the hardest.

**Do I need a training plan to start running?**
Not necessarily. Three runs per week with walk breaks as needed is enough to get going. A formal plan helps more once you have a goal, like a 5K.

**What's the best way to stay motivated as a new runner?**
Put your runs on specific days and times so they don't get negotiated away. Running with other people helps too.

**Should I see a doctor before I start running?**
If you have a heart condition, other medical conditions, or symptoms like chest pain or dizziness with exercise, check with your doctor first.
