If you've ever crossed a finish line and immediately pulled on a pair of compression socks, you already know the move. Compression socks for running are everywhere: race expos, finish-line photos, and the gear bags of runners who swear by them. The question worth asking is whether the evidence supports the hype, or whether this is an accessory that feels like it works without doing much.

The short answer: compression socks probably won't make you faster. The recovery case is less clear than the marketing suggests, but it isn't zero, and the details matter.

## What Compression Socks Are Supposed to Do

The theory is straightforward. Graduated compression, tightest at the ankle and looser up the calf, is meant to help blood move back toward the heart, reduce muscle vibration during impact, and limit swelling after hard efforts. Manufacturers often stretch those claims to include faster recovery and less fatigue.

Some of that is physiologically plausible. Some of it is marketing dressed up as science.

## The Performance Question: Will They Make You Faster?

Probably not. [A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in the Journal of Sport and Health Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39842661/) pulled together 51 eligible studies with 899 participants on compression garments worn during running. It found no significant improvement in race time or time to exhaustion.

A separate [2025 systematic review in the Journal of Sport Rehabilitation](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2024-0410) looked specifically at compression socks during running, including 28 trials with 600 runners. It found that wearing compression socks while running did not change physiological measures, running performance, or perceptual outcomes compared with regular or placebo socks, with evidence ranging from very low to moderate certainty.

If you're pinning your next PR on a pair of compression socks, the research doesn't support that bet.

That doesn't mean you're wrong if your legs feel better in them. Compression research has a known blinding problem: you can feel that you're wearing tight socks, which can shape how you rate effort and soreness. That makes "how my legs feel" a hard outcome to measure fairly.

## The Recovery Question: Possible, but Uncertain

Recovery is where compression has a slightly better case, but it's still thin. [A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in Frontiers in Physiology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.1908148/full) screened 503 records and included nine studies with 158 endurance athletes who wore compression after exercise. At about 24 hours, soreness estimates leaned in favor of compression, but the authors rated the evidence as very low certainty, statistically imprecise, and vulnerable to expectation effects. Effects on later performance were unresolved.

What that means practically: wearing compression socks after a hard run or race might make you feel a little less sore the next day. It might not. The evidence is too small and too uncertain to promise it.

### During the Run vs. After

The research questions are different. Wearing compression during a run is mostly about performance and how your legs feel while running, and reviews haven't found a meaningful benefit there. Wearing compression after a run is about recovery and soreness, where the signal is weak but at least points in a favorable direction.

If you want to experiment, post-run or post-race use is the more reasonable place to start. Some runners wear them both during and after. That's fine, as long as you're not expecting them to do the work of training and rest.

## What mmHg Means (and Why Fit Matters More Than Brand)

Compression is measured in millimeters of mercury (mmHg). [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/what-you-should-know-about-compression-socks) describes common levels as mild (8-15 mmHg), moderate (15-20 mmHg), and firm (20-30 mmHg), with 30-40 mmHg considered extra-firm, medical-grade compression that is usually prescription-only and used under a doctor's supervision. Most running compression socks are sold in the 15-20 or 20-30 mmHg range.

Higher isn't automatically better. Too much compression can be uncomfortable or cause problems. If a sock makes your foot tingle or go numb, or leaves deep marks after a short wear, it's too tight or the wrong size. Take it off.

Fit matters more than most buyers realize. Many compression socks are sized by calf circumference and sometimes ankle circumference, not just shoe size. Measure and check the size chart for the specific brand. A sock that's too loose does little. One that's too tight can create problems.

Calf sleeves, which cover the lower leg but leave the foot bare, are an alternative. They let you wear your preferred running sock underneath, which some runners like for blister management.

## Who Might Want to Try Them

The evidence doesn't support a universal recommendation, but some runners have more reason to experiment.

**Runners in a heavy training block.** If your legs are often sore, post-run compression is a low-risk thing to try. Just don't use it to ignore fatigue that means you need rest.

**Comeback runners.** The first weeks back tend to bring more soreness than you remember. If compression makes the next run feel more inviting, that's a reasonable use, as long as you're also building mileage gradually.

**Runners traveling to races.** Long flights and drives mean a lot of sitting. The [CDC](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/page/dvt) recommends getting up and moving, exercising your calves while seated, and talking to your doctor about compression stockings if you have additional risk factors for blood clots.

**Runners who just like them.** If you've found they help you feel better and you have no side effects, there's no strong reason to stop.

**Pregnant runners.** Compression is sometimes recommended during pregnancy for swelling. If you're running while pregnant, talk to your provider about whether compression makes sense for you and what level to use.

## Potential Downsides

Most healthy runners tolerate compression socks without issue, but there are real cautions.

According to [a review of graduated compression stockings](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4081237/), contraindications include suspected or proven peripheral arterial disease, severe peripheral neuropathy, and certain skin conditions, because compression on legs with poor arterial blood flow can make things worse. If you have circulation problems, diabetes with nerve issues, or any vascular condition, check with your doctor before using them.

Skin irritation and blisters can happen if the fit creates friction points. That's a fit and material problem, but common enough to mention.

And relying on compression to push through constant soreness can mask a training problem. If you're always sore, the fix is usually more rest or a slower build, not tighter socks.

## The Honest Summary

Compression socks for running won't make you faster; the research on performance is consistent enough to set that aside. The recovery case is possible but uncertain, based on a small number of studies that are hard to blind.

What they are is a relatively low-risk tool that some runners like in specific situations. Post-run and travel use make the most sense. Fit and compression level matter more than brand. And if you've tried them and they don't seem to do anything, the research gives you permission to stop.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do compression socks actually improve running performance?**
The evidence says no. A 2025 meta-analysis of 51 studies and 899 participants found no significant improvement in race time or time to exhaustion from compression garments worn while running, and a separate 2025 review of compression socks found no change in performance or physiological outcomes.

**Is there better evidence for recovery than for performance?**
Slightly, but it's weak. A 2026 review of nine studies found soreness at about 24 hours leaned in favor of compression, but the authors rated the evidence as very low certainty.

**Should I wear compression socks during a run or after?**
If you want to try them, after a long run or race is the more reasonable use. Reviews haven't found a performance benefit from wearing them during runs.

**What mmHg level should runners use?**
Most running compression socks are 15-20 or 20-30 mmHg. Higher levels (30-40 mmHg and up) are medical-grade and should only be used with a doctor's guidance.

**Do compression socks or calf sleeves work better for running?**
There isn't strong evidence favoring one over the other. Sleeves let you wear your own running sock underneath; full socks also compress the foot and ankle.

**Are there runners who shouldn't wear compression socks?**
Yes. People with peripheral arterial disease, severe neuropathy, or certain skin conditions should avoid them unless a doctor says otherwise. Anyone who gets numbness, tingling, or deep skin marks should take them off and reassess the size and level.

**Can compression socks replace other recovery strategies?**
No. Sleep, easy days, good nutrition, and gradual training increases are the foundation. Compression socks are optional extras at best.
