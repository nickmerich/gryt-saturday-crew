Searching for a 4 mile run near you? Use a route tool like Google Maps, Strava, or a loop generator to measure or discover nearby paths. Before you go, check the basics: traffic, lighting, and blind corners. If you'd rather skip planning entirely, a local group that already covers about 4 miles is the easiest option.

Most people searching for a 4 mile run want one of three things: a route they can trust, a quick way to build an exact 4 mile loop from their front door, or a local group that removes planning altogether. This article covers all three and what to check before you run.

## Four Ways to Find a 4 Mile Run

**Google Maps** works best when you want full control. You plot the route yourself, watch the distance update, and preview intersections with Street View. It's free.

**Strava** works best when you want routes based on where other people actually run. Its route recommendations and route builder are part of the paid subscription.

**Automated loop generators** like Trail Router work best when you want speed. Enter a distance and a starting point, and the tool builds a loop.

**A local running group** works best when you want to skip planning completely. Someone else has already picked the route and shows up every week.

| Option | Best for | Strength | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|

| Strava | Routes other runners use | Popularity-based route suggestions | Route tools need a subscription |
| Trail Router | Instant loops | Round-trip route at a set distance | Free |

## 1. Use Google Maps to Build an Exact 4 Mile Route

Google Maps is the most reliable free option for hitting exactly 4 miles. Per [Google's help page](https://support.google.com/maps/answer/1628031?hl=en-IE), on a computer you:

1. Open Google Maps and right-click your starting point.
2. Select **Measure distance**.
3. Click along the path you want to run. The total distance appears at the bottom.
4. Drag points to adjust until it reads 4 miles.

On a phone, touch and hold the map to drop a pin, choose **Measure distance**, and add points as you move the map. Measuring doesn't work in Maps Lite mode.

Once you have a route, use Street View to preview it. Look for narrow shoulders, blind corners, and stretches with no sidewalk. A route that looks clean from above can include a stretch of road with no shoulder that you won't notice until you're on it.

The measure tool shows distance, not hills. If your route climbs a lot, it'll feel harder than the number suggests. Run it once as a test before making it a regular loop.

## 2. Use Strava to Find Popular Routes

Strava's route features suggest routes based on where other athletes run, which is a quick way to find paths real runners use. As of October 2026, Strava's [subscription page](https://www.strava.com/subscribe) lists route recommendations and drawing your own routes as subscription features, with the individual annual plan at $79.99 a year in the U.S. The free version still lets you record runs and join clubs.

If you subscribe, search near your starting point, pick a route close to 4 miles, and check what comes up. You'll often find park loops, greenways, or school tracks you wouldn't have mapped yourself.

Before you run any suggested route, check current conditions. Route data shows where people ran, not whether a path is open today. Construction, closures, or storm damage can change things.

## 3. Try a Loop Generator When You Want Speed

When you want a 4 mile loop in under a minute, use a loop generator. [Trail Router](https://trailrouter.com/about/) lets you set a start point and automatically creates a round-trip route at the distance you choose. By default it prefers parks, green space, and water and avoids busy roads where it can, and you can set preferences for lit streets or fewer hills. You can export a GPX file to your watch or running app.

Loop generators solve one specific problem well: getting back to your exact starting point at exactly 4 miles, which takes a lot of dragging to get right by hand.

The tradeoff is local knowledge. A generator doesn't know which intersection has a blind corner. Treat the route as a draft and preview it in Street View before running it alone.

An out-and-back is a simple alternative: run 2 miles out, turn around, and you're done. You know the second half before you run it, and there's no navigation at the turnaround.

## 4. Join a Local Group That Already Runs About 4 Miles

Sometimes the fastest way to find a 4 mile run is to let someone else do the work. Many running stores and community clubs run routes in the 3 to 5 mile range every week.

If you're near Pittsburgh's South Hills, [GRYT Run Club](/) meets every Saturday at 7:00 AM at 212 Valley Brook Road in McMurray, PA. The route is about 4 miles out and back on the Montour/Peters Trail, a flat, paved rail trail, and you can turn around early. Walk, jog, or run. It's no-drop, so the run ends when the last person finishes. It's free, with no membership or pace requirement, and there's coffee in the parking lot after. First-timers RSVP on the website and sign a short liability waiver.

A group fits people who want a set weekly run without planning. It won't help if you need a custom route from your front door on a weeknight, which is where the tools above come in.

## How Long Does a 4 Mile Run Take?

It depends on your pace. The math is simple: multiply your minutes per mile by 4.

| Pace per mile | 4 mile time |
|---|---|
| 8:00 | 32 minutes |
| 10:00 | 40 minutes |
| 12:00 | 48 minutes |
| 15:00 (brisk walk or run-walk) | 60 minutes |
| 18:00 (walk) | 72 minutes |

If you're newer to running, plan around the longer end until you know your pace on that terrain. Hills, heat, and trail surfaces slow everyone down. The [pace calculator](/resources/pace-calculator) can help you work out your numbers.

Route shape matters too. A loop keeps you moving forward. An out-and-back gives you a known halfway point, which helps if you're not sure how you'll feel at mile 2.

## Safety Checklist for a New 4 Mile Route

- **Light:** Check the route at the time you plan to run, not midday. Tree cover can make dawn and dusk darker than you'd expect.
- **Blind corners:** Look for spots where drivers can't see you until you're in the road. Street View catches most of these.
- **Crossings:** Count how often you cross traffic. Fewer is better.
- **Shoulders and sidewalks:** A road with no shoulder and no sidewalk is a bigger risk than a paved trail or greenway.
- **Surface:** Uneven ground raises the chance of rolled ankles, especially in low light.
- **Bail-out option:** Know a shortcut back to your start in case you need to stop early.
- **Tell someone:** Share your route and expected finish time, especially on a new route.

If you run before sunrise or after dark, the [running at night safety guide](/resources/running-at-night-safety-pittsburgh) covers lights, visibility, and route choice in more detail.

## FAQs

### What's a good 4 mile route for beginners?

A paved greenway, rail trail, or park loop with few traffic crossings. You want a flat or gently rolling surface, good visibility, and few navigation decisions mid-run.

### Is a loop or an out-and-back better for a 4 mile run?

Both work. A loop keeps you moving forward but takes more navigation on a new route. An out-and-back is simpler: 2 miles out, turn around, done.

### How do I make sure a route is exactly 4 miles?

Use Google Maps' Measure distance tool and adjust points until it reads 4 miles. If you use a loop generator, double-check the distance in Google Maps. GPS watches can also read a little long or short, especially under trees.

### Are greenways better than neighborhood roads for a 4 mile run?

For most runners, yes. Greenways and rail trails separate you from traffic and usually have consistent surfaces. The main tradeoff is that you have to get to the trailhead first.

### What should I check before running at dawn or dusk?

Visit at the time you'll run, look for lighting and open sightlines, and wear a light and reflective gear. Know where the blind corners and no-shoulder stretches are.

### Can I find a free 4 mile run near me?

Yes. Google Maps and Trail Router are free for building routes. Many local groups are free to join, including GRYT Run Club in McMurray, PA, which runs about 4 miles every Saturday.

## Conclusion

Finding a 4 mile run near you comes down to how much planning you want to do. Google Maps gives you exact control for free. Strava suggests routes other runners use if you subscribe. Trail Router builds a loop in seconds. A local group removes every decision except whether to show up.

Pick the option that fits your schedule. If you're building a route from scratch, preview it in Street View, run through the safety checklist, and have a bail-out option on the first try.
