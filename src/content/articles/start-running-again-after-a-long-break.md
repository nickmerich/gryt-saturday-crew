Sports medicine guidance on starting to run again after a long break is consistent: walk first, then alternate short jogs with walking two or three times a week, and build up over about six weeks. The easiest way to stick with it is a no-drop group where walking is normal, so nobody is watching your pace.

That's the short version. Below you'll find a quick readiness check, a six-week walk-run plan from a published physiotherapy program, a guide to which aches are fine, and a checklist for picking a group that won't judge you. This is general information, not medical advice. If you have a health condition, a recent injury, or you've been inactive for a long time, check with your doctor before you start.

## Step 1: Make Sure You're Ready

A simple test comes from [Berkshire Healthcare NHS's return to running programme](https://www.berkshirehealthcare.nhs.uk/media/2jmpfnad/return-to-running-bh1165q-feb-2026.pdf), a physiotherapy handout written for people coming back from injury and reviewed in February 2026. It says you should generally be able to walk for 30 minutes and get through your day-to-day demands with no symptoms, or a low level of symptoms, before you start running.

If you're not there yet, walking is the plan for now, and it counts. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/adults.html) recommends 150 minutes of moderate activity a week for adults, and it's clear that "some physical activity is better than none."

## Step 2: Wake Up the Muscles First

Dr. Morgan Busko, a sports medicine physician at NewYork-Presbyterian/Columbia University Irving Medical Center, told [NewYork-Presbyterian's Health Matters](https://www.nyp.org/healthmatters/how-to-start-running-after-a-long-break-according-to-a-sports-medicine-doctor) that returning runners should have a basic strength routine under way before they start running. Her suggestions are simple resistance-band moves:

- Clamshells
- Monster walks
- Glute bridges
- Dead bugs

According to the article, even five to 10 minutes a few times a week helps. She also recommends a dynamic warm-up of five to 15 minutes, with leg swings, hip mobility work, and shoulder and torso rotations, and saving static stretching for after the run.

If you want a place to start, GRYT has a [10-minute warm-up routine](/resources/articles/running-warm-up-routine) and [six strength moves for runners](/resources/articles/strength-training-for-runners).

## Step 3: Follow a Six-Week Walk-Run Plan

The NewYork-Presbyterian article puts the main risk plainly: "One of the biggest mistakes returning runners make is doing too much too soon." Dr. Busko's fix is a walk-run approach. In her words, "it can be very helpful to jog a minute, walk a minute, jog a minute, walk a minute."

Berkshire Healthcare NHS lays that idea out week by week. Do each session two to three times a week, with 24 hours of rest between runs.

| Week | Session |
|---|---|
| 1 | Jog 1 minute, walk 1 minute, for 30 minutes total |
| 2 | Jog 2 minutes, walk 1 minute, for 30 minutes total |
| 3 | Jog 4 minutes, walk 1 minute, for 30 minutes total |
| 4 | Jog 9 minutes, walk 1 minute, for 30 minutes total |
| 5 | Jog 15 minutes, walk 1 minute |
| 6 | Jog 30 minutes without stopping |
| 7 and on | Add no more than 10% per week. If you ran 30 minutes this week, run 33 next week |

A few notes on using it:

- **It's a guide, not a deadline.** The handout was written for people working with a physiotherapist. Repeat any week until it feels comfortable.
- **There's a gentler option.** The [NHS Couch to 5K plan](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/get-running-with-couch-to-5k/) has you running three times a week with rest days in between. Week one is 1-minute runs followed by 90 seconds of walking, and the NHS says you can finish it in nine weeks or longer.
- **Keep it easy.** Jog at a pace where you could hold a conversation. Our guide to [conversational pace](/resources/articles/conversational-pace-running) explains the talk test.

## Step 4: Know Which Aches Are Fine

Some soreness is normal when you come back. The Berkshire Healthcare NHS handout splits it into two groups:

| Fine, keep progressing | Not fine, back off |
|---|---|
| General muscle soreness after a session | Pain that lasts more than 48 hours after a session |
| Slight muscle or joint discomfort that's gone within 24 hours | Pain at the start of a session that gets worse as you go |
| Slight stiffness at the start that fades in the first 10 minutes | Persistent swelling in a joint, such as a knee or ankle |
|  | Sharp pain |

If things are getting worse, take a break and talk to your doctor or a physical therapist. The handout notes that this doesn't mean it's anything too serious. You may just need some extra help.

## Step 5: Pick a Group That Won't Judge Your Pace

The NewYork-Presbyterian article makes a point most returning runners already know: the biggest challenge often isn't physical, it's getting back out the door. A group helps with that, as long as it's the right kind of group. Look for these six things:

- **A no-drop rule.** Nobody gets left behind, and the run isn't over until the last person is back.
- **Walkers welcome.** If walking is normal, walk-run intervals are normal too.
- **An out-and-back route.** You can turn around at any point and still finish with everyone else.
- **No pace requirement and no clock.** Nobody asks what you run a mile in.
- **The same time and place every week.** You don't have to make a new decision each time.
- **Something after.** Coffee in a parking lot does more for consistency than a training plan.

Before your first visit, ask three questions:

1. What happens to the slowest person?
2. Can I walk part of it, or turn around early?
3. Is there a pace I'm expected to hold?

If the answers are vague, keep looking. Our guides to finding a [beginner running group near you](/resources/articles/beginner-running-group-near-me) and a [slow runners club](/resources/articles/slow-runners-club-near-me) cover where to search.

## Your First Group Run Back

1. **Say it's your first run back.** Tell the organizer when you sign up or when you arrive. At GRYT, first-timers RSVP on the site and sign a short waiver, and a founder or regular will find you before the start.
2. **Get there 10 minutes early.** Meeting two people before the run makes the first mile easier.
3. **Run your intervals, not the group's pace.** If this is a week-two day, it's still jog 2, walk 1. A good group won't care.
4. **Pick your turnaround before you start.** On an out-and-back, turn around at half your planned time.
5. **Stay for coffee.** The people you meet afterward are the reason you'll come back next week.

## FAQs

**How long does it take to get back into running after a long break?**
On the Berkshire Healthcare NHS walk-run plan, about six weeks to reach 30 minutes of nonstop jogging. The NHS Couch to 5K plan takes nine weeks or longer. Repeat a week whenever you need to.

**Should I walk before I start running again?**
Yes. The Berkshire Healthcare NHS readiness test is being able to walk for 30 minutes and get through your day with no symptoms, or only mild ones.

**How many days a week should I run when I'm starting over?**
Two to three, with a rest day between runs. Add a few short strength sessions on other days.

**Will a run club judge me for being slow?**
A good one won't. Look for a no-drop rule, walkers welcome, and no pace requirement. At GRYT Run Club, the run ends when the last person finishes, and walking counts.

**Is it OK to walk during a group run?**
Yes. A walk-run approach is what the sports medicine and NHS sources above recommend for coming back, and plenty of groups have walkers every week.

**When should I see a doctor?**
Check in before you start if you have a health condition or you've been inactive for a long time. Once you're running, stop and get help for sharp pain, pain that lasts more than 48 hours, pain that gets worse as you run, or swelling in a joint.
