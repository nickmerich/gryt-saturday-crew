More stack height does not automatically mean a better run. Cushioned running shoes have dominated shelves and social feeds for years, and the marketing logic sounds reasonable: more foam equals less impact, less impact equals fewer injuries. The real picture is less tidy than that.

Here's what the research and everyday running suggest about cushion, and how to figure out what your feet actually need.

## What "Max Cushion" Actually Means

There's no official definition, but "max cushion" usually describes shoes with a very tall midsole, often in the mid-30s of millimeters or higher. For context, [World Athletics rules](https://worldathletics.org/download/download?filename=61b09e88-36c9-4782-8563-2adce1e15ff0.pdf&urlslug=C2.1A%20%E2%80%93%20Athletic%20Shoe%20Regulations%20(effective%20from%2020%20April%202026)) cap sole thickness at 40 mm for shoes used in elite road races, which is why you'll see that number come up. Plenty of training shoes sit close to it.

The category took off after [HOKA launched in 2009](https://www.hoka.com/en/md/meet-hoka.html) with an oversized, high-cushion trail shoe, followed by the Bondi road shoe in 2010. Now nearly every major brand has a max-cushion option.

Stack height, foam firmness, and how cushioned a shoe feels are three different things. A shoe can have a tall stack and feel firm. Another can have a moderate stack and feel plush. What you experience underfoot depends on the foam, the shoe's shape, and your own mechanics.

## What the Research Says

The evidence on cushioning and injury is limited, but there is some. In [a 2020 randomized trial of 848 recreational runners in the American Journal of Sports Medicine](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0363546519892578), runners who got harder-cushioned shoes had a higher injury risk than runners in softer versions of the same shoe. The difference showed up mainly in lighter runners.

That's one study with prototype shoes, so it isn't a mandate to buy the softest shoe you can find. It does suggest cushioning isn't irrelevant, and that the right amount may depend on the runner.

## The Case for Cushioned Running Shoes

There are real reasons to reach for a cushioned shoe.

**Higher-mileage weeks.** When you're running a lot, many runners find a softer ride makes back-to-back days feel more manageable, especially while they're still adapting to the volume.

**Road running on hard surfaces.** If most of your miles are on sidewalks or asphalt, extra cushion is a reasonable comfort choice. The Montour/Peters Trail in McMurray is flat and paved, so it rides more like a road than a dirt trail.

**Recovery runs.** Plenty of experienced runners keep a softer shoe for easy days. The goal on those runs is easy movement, and a plush shoe can make tired legs feel better.

**Older and comeback runners.** Many runners find their feet appreciate more cushion as they get older or as they return after a long break. If a softer shoe makes it easier to stay consistent, that's a good reason to wear it.

## The Case Against Defaulting to Max Cushion

A thicker midsole raises your foot farther from the ground. Some runners feel less connected to the surface and less stable in very tall shoes, particularly when cornering or on uneven ground.

Very soft, tall shoes can also feel wobbly for some people. If your ankles feel unsteady or you find yourself rolling outward, the shoe may not be the right match, no matter how comfortable it felt in the store.

**Stability is not the same as cushion.** Some runners buy max-cushion shoes thinking more foam means more support. It doesn't. If you want more guidance or structure, look at stability models rather than assuming the tallest neutral shoe will do the job.

## Trail Running and Cushion: A Different Calculation

On trails, the cushion question shifts. Roots, rocks, loose gravel, and packed dirt change constantly, and your foot needs to respond to all of it.

On technical terrain, a very tall stack can make it harder to feel the ground and react quickly. Many trail shoes prioritize grip, a protective upper, and sometimes a rock plate, though max-cushion trail shoes exist too and some runners love them for long days.

The Montour Trail is not technical singletrack. The Peters Township stretch where GRYT runs is paved and predictable, so a regular road shoe works well there. You don't need a trail shoe at all for that kind of run.

## How to Actually Decide

Skip the marketing. Ask yourself four questions.

**What surface are you running on?** Hard road miles make extra cushion more appealing. Technical trails favor grip and stability.

**What is your weekly mileage?** At lower mileage, cushion is mostly a comfort preference. As your volume climbs, comfort over many consecutive days matters more.

**Do you have an injury history?** Past injuries are worth talking through with a physical therapist or a knowledgeable running store fitter. Different problems can respond differently to cushioning and heel-to-toe drop, and a professional can look at your specific situation.

**How do your legs feel after an hour?** If you're beat up late in runs in a moderate shoe, try something softer. If you feel disconnected from the ground or unsteady in a max-cushion shoe, go down a notch.

Run in a few different options and pay attention to how you feel the next day, not just during the run.

## Rotating Shoes Is Not Just a Marketing Gimmick

One practical answer to the cushion question is to stop treating it as either-or. Many runners keep two pairs: a cushioned shoe for long runs and easy days, and a firmer, lighter shoe for faster or shorter efforts.

There's some evidence behind this. In [a 2015 study of 264 recreational runners in the Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24286345/), runners who used more than one pair of shoes in parallel had about a 39% lower risk of running-related injury than those who mostly used one pair. It was an observational study, so it can't prove the shoes caused the difference, but varying the load across runs is a plausible explanation.

This isn't about spending money for its own sake. If one good pair fits your budget, that's fine.

## What Cushion Cannot Fix

No shoe fixes a training error. Ramping up too quickly, skipping recovery, and ignoring early warning signs are common drivers of running injuries. A soft shoe can make a run feel easier while the underlying problem builds.

If you're [building a running base](/resources/build-a-running-base) or returning after time off, the progression matters more than the shoe. A gradual build in an inexpensive trainer beats a reckless mileage jump in the most expensive shoe on the wall. If something hurts and doesn't settle down with a few easy days, see a doctor or physical therapist.

Cushion is a variable, not a solution.

## FAQs

**Are cushioned running shoes better for beginners?**
Not automatically. Many beginners do well in a moderate-cushion shoe that feels stable and comfortable. The more important variables are running at an easy pace and building mileage gradually. Comfort and fit should guide the choice.

**Do cushioned running shoes prevent injuries?**
The evidence is limited. One randomized trial found a higher injury risk in harder shoes than softer ones, mainly among lighter runners. But no shoe prevents injuries on its own. Training load and recovery are bigger factors.

**What is a good stack height for everyday running?**
There's no single right number. Many everyday trainers fall somewhere in the high 20s to mid 30s of millimeters, and max-cushion shoes go higher. Let comfort, stability, and your usual surface guide you.

**Can I use a max-cushion shoe for trail running?**
On smooth, maintained trails, yes. On technical trails with roots and rocks, a very tall, soft shoe can feel less stable, so many runners prefer something lower with better grip. Match the shoe to the terrain.

**Is it worth rotating between two pairs of running shoes?**
It can be. An observational study found runners who used more than one pair had a lower injury risk, and rotating can extend the life of each pair. It's helpful but not required.

**How do I know if my shoes have too much cushion for me?**
Watch for ankle wobble, a disconnected feeling, or an unsteady landing. If you feel more controlled in a moderate shoe, that's your answer.

**How long do cushioned running shoes last?**
The [American Academy of Podiatric Sports Medicine](http://www.aapsm.org/replace-shoes.html) suggests replacing a typical running shoe every 350 to 500 miles, sooner for heavier runners. Track your mileage and replace based on feel, not appearance.
