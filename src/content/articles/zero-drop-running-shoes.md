Zero drop running shoes split runners into two camps: people who love them and people who got hurt switching too fast. Knowing which camp you're likely to land in before you buy can save you a frustrating stretch of sore calves and a lot of wasted money.

"Zero drop" means the heel and forefoot sit at the same height. Many traditional running shoes raise the heel roughly 8 to 12 millimeters above the forefoot. That difference affects how your foot meets the ground and how load is shared between your calf, Achilles, and knee.

It's a meaningful design choice, not automatically better or worse. It depends on who you are and how you run.

## What Zero Drop Actually Changes

A raised heel makes it easier to land heel-first. Lowering the heel tends to nudge many runners toward a flatter or more midfoot landing, which shifts more work to the calf and Achilles and less to the knee.

That shift is the main argument for zero drop. Supporters also like the flatter, more stable platform and the feeling of being closer to the ground.

The argument against it is just as simple: your body has adapted to whatever shoes you've worn for years. Change the geometry and you change the load pattern. Tissue that hasn't been conditioned for that load needs time to catch up.

## What the Research Says

The best-known trial on this question is [a 2016 randomized controlled trial in the American Journal of Sports Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27501833/) that gave 553 recreational runners cushioned shoes with a 10 mm, 6 mm, or 0 mm drop and followed them for six months. Overall, drop did not change injury risk. When the researchers split the group, though, low-drop shoes were linked to lower injury risk in occasional runners and higher injury risk in regular runners.

That's one study, not the final word, but it lines up with the practical advice: if you've been running consistently in a traditional shoe, a sudden switch to zero drop is a real change for your legs.

## Who May Benefit from Zero Drop Shoes

### Runners Who Already Land Midfoot

If you already land on your midfoot or forefoot, a zero drop shoe may feel natural because it isn't asking your body to change much.

### Some Runners with Knee Pain

Shifting load from the knee toward the ankle is one reason some runners with knee pain experiment with lower-drop shoes. It isn't a reliable fix, and moving load to the calf and Achilles can create a different problem. If you have ongoing knee pain, see a doctor or physical therapist before treating a shoe change as the solution.

### Runners Who Want More Ground Feel

Zero drop shoes, especially lower-stack versions, can give you a clearer sense of the surface under your feet. Some runners simply prefer that feel.

### Runners with Moderate Mileage

The extra calf and Achilles demand is easier to manage at moderate volume. If your typical run is 3 to 5 miles a few times a week, a gradual transition is more manageable than it is for someone in the middle of a high-mileage training block.

## Who Should Be Careful

### Regular Runners with High Weekly Mileage

If you've been running consistently in a traditional-drop shoe and you're putting in a lot of miles, a switch to zero drop is a significant change at high volume. That's the group where the 2016 trial saw higher injury risk with low-drop shoes. Go slowly.

### Comeback Runners

Comeback runners are in a vulnerable spot. Fitness often returns faster than tendons and connective tissue adapt, so you may feel ready for more than your legs can handle. Stacking a zero drop transition on top of a return-to-running ramp means two big adaptations at once. Pick one.

If you're rebuilding right now, [how to build a running base](/resources/build-a-running-base) covers the progression. Get the mileage foundation solid before experimenting with shoe geometry.

### Runners with Achilles, Calf, or Plantar History

A past Achilles problem, plantar fasciitis, or calf strain is a reason for caution. Zero drop puts more demand on those structures. That doesn't mean zero drop is off the table forever. It means the transition should be slower, and it's worth checking with a physical therapist first.

### Older Runners

Many runners find that tendons and connective tissue take longer to adapt with age. That's not a reason to avoid zero drop. It's a reason to give yourself a longer timeline than a generic plan suggests.

## The Transition Problem

Many zero drop problems come less from the shoes and more from how fast people switch.

The classic mistake: a runner buys a pair and immediately runs their normal distance in them, then ends up with sore calves or an irritated Achilles a few weeks later. Research on minimalist shoes points the same way. In [a 2013 study in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3042&context=facpub), 10 of 19 runners who gradually moved into minimalist shoes over 10 weeks showed increased foot bone marrow edema (a sign of bone stress) on MRI, significantly more than a group that stayed in traditional shoes.

A conservative transition might look like this:

- **Weeks 1-2:** Wear the zero drop shoes for short, easy runs only, around 20 to 30 minutes at most. Keep your regular shoes for everything else.
- **Weeks 3-4:** If you feel fine, use them for a modest share of your weekly running, roughly a quarter to a third.
- **Weeks 5-8:** Gradually shift the balance. Pay close attention to calf tightness and Achilles sensitivity.
- **Week 9 onward:** Use them as much as you like if the previous weeks produced no warning signs.

Treat that as a minimum for a healthy runner with no lower-leg history. If you're coming back from time off or have had Achilles or calf issues, stretch it out further.

Strength work helps. Calf raises, single-leg variations, and slow lowering (eccentric) work prepare the calf and Achilles for more demand. If you're adding supplemental work between runs, this is a good time to prioritize it. The [cross-training guide](/resources/cross-training-for-runners) covers low-impact options for the days in between.

## Zero Drop vs. Minimalist: Not the Same Thing

Zero drop and minimalist are related but different. Zero drop refers only to the heel-to-toe drop. A shoe can be zero drop and still have plenty of cushioning. [Altra](https://www.altrarunning.com/en-us/collections/zero-drop-shoes), for example, makes most of its running shoes zero drop, including cushioned road models like the Torin and Olympus, and pairs them with a wide, foot-shaped toe box.

Minimalist shoes reduce both the drop and the cushioning. Thinner, lighter, more ground feel, but less buffer between your foot and the surface.

For most runners trying zero drop for the first time, starting with a cushioned zero drop model is smarter than going straight to a thin minimalist shoe. Change one variable at a time.

## What to Watch for After Switching

Your body will tell you if the transition is moving too fast. Take these signals seriously:

- **Calf tightness that doesn't ease within a day** of an easy run
- **Achilles soreness** at the back of the heel, especially first thing in the morning
- **Arch or heel pain** that lingers more than a couple of days
- **Shin pain** that feels different from normal training fatigue

Any of these is a reason to back off, not push through. General muscle soreness is normal when you change shoes; tendon pain and bone pain are not. If pain persists or gets worse, stop the transition and see a doctor or physical therapist.

## The Honest Assessment

Zero drop works well for some runners: people with reasonable lower-leg strength, moderate weekly mileage, and the patience to spend a couple of months transitioning. It's not a fix for injuries, it's not universally better than traditional shoes, and it's not the right experiment for a comeback runner still rebuilding weekly volume.

The right shoe is the one you can train in consistently, without injury, for months at a time. Zero drop might be that shoe for you. It might not be. The way to find out is to transition slowly and pay attention.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are zero drop running shoes better for your feet?**
Not universally. They may suit runners who already land midfoot or who want a flatter platform. Whether they're better for you depends on your mechanics, injury history, and how gradually you transition. In one randomized trial, low-drop shoes were linked to lower injury risk in occasional runners but higher risk in regular runners.

**Can zero drop shoes cause Achilles problems?**
They can if the transition is too fast. Lowering the heel puts more demand on the calf and Achilles. The tissue needs time to adapt, so build up gradually and back off if you feel Achilles pain. Persistent pain is worth a visit to a doctor or physical therapist.

**How long does it take to transition to zero drop shoes?**
Plan on at least eight weeks for a healthy runner with no lower-leg injury history. Start with short easy runs, keep your old shoes for most of your mileage, and shift the balance slowly. Comeback runners, older runners, and anyone with past Achilles or calf issues should take longer.

**What is the difference between zero drop and minimalist running shoes?**
Zero drop only means the heel and forefoot are the same height; the shoe can still be well cushioned. Minimalist shoes reduce both drop and cushioning. If you're new to zero drop, start with a cushioned model.

**Should comeback runners try zero drop shoes?**
Not during the initial return phase. Get your base mileage consistent first, then consider a gradual shoe change.

**Do zero drop shoes help with knee pain?**
For some runners, maybe. Lowering the heel can shift load from the knee toward the ankle, but it isn't a reliable fix and can create new calf or Achilles issues. If you have ongoing knee pain, start with a doctor or physical therapist.

**Can I wear zero drop shoes for trail running?**
Yes. Several zero drop trail shoes exist, and some runners like the ground feel on uneven surfaces. The same transition rules apply: start short, build slowly, and pay attention to your calves and Achilles.
