Jogging clubs and running clubs both get you outside and moving with other people. But they feel different, cost differently, and attract different crowds. Your pace, your goals, and how much structure you want will point you toward one or the other. The quick answer: start with a jogging club if you are new or returning, and choose a running club if you have a race goal or want coached workouts.

There are plenty of people to run with. Running and jogging passed 50 million US participants in 2024 for the first time since 2020, according to [the Sports & Fitness Industry Association's 2025 Topline Participation Report](https://sfia.org/resources/sfias-topline-participation-report-shows-247-1-million-americans-were-active-in-2024/). Group running is growing fast too: [Strava's 2024 Year in Sport report](https://press.strava.com/articles/strava-releases-annual-year-in-sport-trend) counted a 59% increase in running club participation on its platform. The question is which kind of club fits your life right now.

## Key Differences at a Glance

The two club types overlap a lot but split on structure, pace expectations, and cost.

| Feature | Jogging Club | Running Club |
|---|---|---|
| Typical pace focus | Conversational, easy effort | Varied, often includes tempo and race-pace work |
| Typical cost | Usually free or very low cost | Free to paid; some structured clubs charge annual dues |
| Structure | Informal, social-first, often no-drop | Often includes training plans, coached workouts, or race prep |
| Primary goal | Consistency, connection, enjoyment | Performance, race goals, or both |
| Who it suits best | Beginners, returning runners, social-first movers | Runners chasing a PR, training for a race, or wanting structured progression |

## What Is a Jogging Club?

A jogging club is a low-pressure group that shows up regularly, runs at a pace where you can hold a conversation, and keeps everyone together. In a no-drop group, nobody gets left behind. The run ends when the last person finishes, not when the fastest one does.

Pace in these groups varies widely, and most don't post a target pace. Expect the organizer to tell you the route and where to meet afterward, not how fast to go.

Jogging club membership is almost always free or close to it. There are no training plans, no race registrations baked in, and no pressure to show up every single week. That low barrier is the point.

The social side has real health value. In [a 12-week study of medical students published in The Journal of the American Osteopathic Association](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29084328/), people who exercised in a group class reported a 26% drop in perceived stress, while those who worked out alone saw no significant change in stress ([ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/10/171030092917.htm)). It was a small study and the group workout wasn't running, but it fits what a lot of people notice: showing up with others makes exercise feel better.

Jogging clubs suit beginners, comeback runners, people easing back after an injury (with their doctor or physical therapist's OK), and anyone who wants a standing weekly appointment without a training cycle behind it.

## What Is a Running Club?

Running clubs cover a wider range of formats, from casual weekend groups to coached training programs with structured workouts, pace groups, and race calendars. The defining feature is that performance improvement is usually part of the conversation.

Most running clubs still welcome newer runners. But the culture tends to reward showing up prepared, hitting target paces, and racing. If a jogging club's main goal is connection, a running club's is progression. Those two aren't mutually exclusive, but the emphasis shifts.

Community still matters in running clubs, through post-run hangouts, group chats, and shared race days. You just get it alongside a training calendar.

Membership cost varies. Many running clubs are free. Others charge annual dues to cover insurance, coaching, or event access.

The Road Runners Club of America (RRCA), founded in 1958, describes itself as [the oldest and largest national association of runners and running organizations](https://www.rrca.org/about/). Many US clubs are RRCA members, and RRCA also certifies running coaches. Membership is a sign a club has some organizational structure behind it.

## Which Type of Club Is Right for You?

Use this table to match your situation to a club type.

| Your situation | Better fit |
|---|---|
| You are new to running or returning after a break | Jogging club |
| You want to train for a specific race | Running club |
| You want to show up without a plan and just move | Jogging club |
| You want coached workouts or a structured training cycle | Running club |
| Cost is a concern | Jogging club (usually free) |
| You want to train with people at a similar competitive level | Running club |
| You want everyone to finish together | Jogging club |
| You want pace groups and performance tracking | Running club |

People tend to quit when a commitment doesn't match their current life. Picking the wrong club type is the same trap. A jogging club keeps you showing up because the bar stays low. A running club keeps you showing up because the goals pull you forward.

If you are unsure, start with a jogging club. You can always add structure later. Rebuilding the habit after burning out on a training plan you weren't ready for is much harder. The club you actually attend beats a competitive club you drop after six weeks.

## How to Find a Run Club Near You

Finding a club usually takes less time than you'd think. Here is where to start.

**Search Instagram and Strava.** Run clubs post weekly meetup details and group photos on Instagram, and many keep a club page on Strava. Search your city plus "run club" and look for accounts with recent, consistent posts.

**Check specialty running stores.** Many local running stores host weekly group runs. A store run is one of the easiest ways to try a group, and staff usually know the local scene and can point you toward clubs that match your pace.

**Use the RRCA club finder.** The [RRCA's Find a Running Club page](https://www.rrca.org/clubs/) lists member clubs by state.

**Ask at a local race.** Race-day expos and finish areas are where club members gather. Talk to people wearing club shirts.

## FAQs

### Is a run club good for meeting people or dating?

Run clubs are a natural way to meet people because the activity gives you something to talk about right away. In [Strava's 2024 Year in Sport survey](https://press.strava.com/articles/strava-releases-annual-year-in-sport-trend), 58% of respondents said they had made new friends through fitness groups. Many clubs build in post-run coffee or brunch, which is where most of the socializing happens. If meeting people is a priority, look for clubs that advertise social time after the run.

### What is the average pace of a social run club?

Social run clubs rarely post a single target pace. Most aim for a conversational effort: you can talk in full sentences without gasping. That covers a wide range of actual paces depending on who shows up. The best way to know is to ask the organizer or show up once and see. A no-drop club will adjust to whoever shows up that day.

### What is a queer run club?

Queer run clubs are LGBTQ+-inclusive running groups built around safety, belonging, and community for queer runners. They work like other social run clubs, with regular meetups, group runs, and social time after. The difference is intentional: the culture is built to make LGBTQ+ runners feel specifically welcome. Many cities have one, often through the Front Runners network. Search Instagram or ask at a local running store.

### Do I need to be fast to join a running club?

No. Most running clubs, and nearly all jogging clubs, welcome any pace. Clubs with pace requirements usually say so up front, often because they're training groups built around a specific race. If a club doesn't list a minimum pace, assume it's open, and ask about the typical effort level before the run starts.

### What is the 80% rule in running?

It's the idea that most of your weekly running should be easy, with a smaller share at hard efforts. A [review of training intensity in endurance athletes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4621419/) notes that elite endurance athletes do roughly 80% of their training at low intensity. Easy miles build your aerobic base without piling up fatigue. If you can't hold a conversation, you're working harder than an easy day calls for.

## Conclusion

Jogging clubs prioritize showing up together at a pace that feels good. Running clubs add structure, training goals, and often a race calendar. Both deliver real social and health benefits. The difference is what you want from the experience right now.

If you are new, returning, or just want a consistent weekly reason to move, start with a jogging club. If you have a race on the calendar or want coached progression, look for a structured running club. Either way, pick one, show up twice, and let the habit form before you decide if it fits.
