The first few minutes of a run are often the stiffest. You go from sitting in the car or at a desk to asking your hips, knees, and ankles to handle running, and it can take a while for everything to loosen up.

A simple running warm-up routine takes about 10 minutes. It's not glamorous, and it won't feel like a workout. But it can make the start of your run feel smoother and help you settle into your stride sooner.

Here's a practical, repeatable routine you can do in a parking lot, at a trailhead, or in your driveway before any run.

## Why Warming Up Is Worth It

A warm-up gradually raises your body temperature and heart rate and moves your joints through the range of motion you're about to use. That tends to make the first part of a run feel easier than going from zero to full stride.

This matters even more if you're running early in the morning or after a long day at a desk. Your body has been mostly still for hours, and a gradual on-ramp helps.

The goal isn't to tire yourself out. It's to warm up a little, wake up the muscles you'll use, and move through your full range before the real work begins.

## What the Research Says About Stretching

You'll hear strong claims about warm-ups and injuries. The research is more mixed than many running articles suggest.

A large [systematic review by Behm and colleagues in Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26642915/) found that long static stretches (60 seconds or more per muscle group) before activity led to bigger short-term performance drops than shorter holds, while dynamic stretching tended to cause small improvements. The same review found stretching had no clear effect on overall or overuse injury rates, though there was some evidence it may help reduce muscle strain injuries in running and sprinting.

The practical takeaway: before a run, favor movement over long static holds. Think of a warm-up as a way to feel ready and run better, not as a guaranteed injury shield.

## The 10-Minute Routine

This sequence is built around dynamic movement. If you like static stretching, keep holds short before your run and save longer stretches for after.

### Minutes 1-2: Easy Walking or Slow March

Start with two minutes of brisk walking or an exaggerated marching walk. Swing your arms, lift your knees a little higher than normal, and pay attention to your posture. This is just about getting moving and warming up.

On cold mornings, this also gives you time to adjust to the air before you pick up the pace.

### Minutes 3-4: Leg Swings

Find something to hold for balance, like a fence post, a car bumper, or a tree.

**Forward and back:** Swing one leg forward and back in a controlled arc, 10 reps per side. Keep your upper body still. This loosens up your hip flexors and hamstrings.

**Side to side:** Swing the same leg across your body and out to the side, 10 reps per side. This gets your hips moving side to side, which helps with the balance you need on one foot during every running stride.

### Minutes 5-6: Hip Circles and Ankle Rolls

**Hip circles:** Stand with feet about shoulder-width apart and draw large circles with your hips, 10 in each direction.

**Ankle rolls:** Lift one foot slightly off the ground and rotate your ankle in slow circles, 10 in each direction per foot. Ankles are easy to forget in a warm-up, and this is especially nice before running on uneven ground.

### Minutes 7-8: Bodyweight Squats and Lunges

**Squats:** 10 slow, controlled reps. Sit back into your hips and keep your weight spread through your whole foot. This wakes up your glutes and quads.

**Walking lunges:** 10 steps per leg. Keep your front knee tracking over your foot. This mirrors the single-leg loading of running.

### Minutes 9-10: High Knees and Butt Kicks

These two moves bridge the gap between your warm-up and your run.

**High knees:** About 20 seconds at a comfortable pace. Lift your knees while pumping your arms. This raises your heart rate a bit more.

**Butt kicks:** About 20 seconds. Kick your heels toward your glutes as you move forward. This loosens up your hamstrings and quads.

After that, start your run at an easy pace for the first few minutes.

## A Few Things Worth Knowing

**Cold mornings call for more time, not less.** On cold days, add an extra minute or two of walking and move through each drill a little slower. For layering ideas, see [what to wear running](/resources/what-to-wear-running).

**An easy first mile counts too.** Starting your run slowly is a perfectly good warm-up, and many runners do just that. The drills add mobility and help you feel loose sooner, so combine the two if you have time.

**Consistency beats perfection.** You don't need to nail every rep. A five-minute version is better than nothing.

**Pain is different from stiffness.** Stiffness that eases as you warm up is common. Pain that gets worse as you move, or that sticks around after runs, is a reason to stop and talk to a doctor or physical therapist.

## After Your Run: The Cool-Down Side

After your run, walk for a few minutes before you sit down or get in the car. If static stretching feels good, this is a good time for it: hold a hip flexor stretch and a calf stretch against a curb or step for about 30 seconds per side.

That combination, movement before and optional stretching after, is a sensible routine for most everyday runners. ## FAQs

**How long should a running warm-up be?**
About 10 minutes works for most runs. If it's cold or you've been sitting most of the day, you might stretch it to 12 to 15 minutes with extra walking and slower drills.

**Can I just stretch before running instead of doing a dynamic warm-up?**
Short static stretches are fine, but long holds before activity can temporarily reduce performance, according to a large research review. Dynamic movements like leg swings, lunges, and high knees are a better fit before a run.

**What happens if I skip the warm-up?**
For an easy run, starting slowly for the first several minutes works as a built-in warm-up. Research hasn't shown a clear link between warm-up stretching and overall injury rates, but a gradual start usually makes the run feel better. Warming up matters more before faster or harder efforts.

**Do I need to warm up for short runs too?**
It's a good idea, especially for short, fast runs. A hard 2-mile effort asks more of your body right away than a relaxed long run, so a few minutes of easy jogging and drills helps.

**Should I warm up differently for trail running versus road running?**
The core routine is the same, but you might add extra ankle rolls and hip circles before heading onto uneven ground.

**Is it okay to do the warm-up inside and then run outside?**
Yes. Doing the routine indoors on a cold morning is fine. Start your run at an easy pace once you step outside, since you'll cool off a little in between.

**What if I only have five minutes before a group run starts?**
Prioritize leg swings, hip circles, and a set of walking lunges, then start the run easy. Five focused minutes beats a rushed ten.

Ten minutes before your run is a small investment. Your stride will loosen up faster and the first mile will feel better. Keep it simple, do it often, and it'll become automatic.
